# Discovery to Proposal Deck

This deck sits between discovery and a signed scope. Its job is not to sell a solution but to prove that the problem was heard correctly and to propose a shape for the work that the client can react to. A client who reads the first section and thinks "yes, that is our situation" will argue about the approach rather than about whether you understood them, which is the only useful argument to be having.

## Gather before drafting

Discovery notes or transcripts, the client's own phrasing for the problem, who was in the room and what each of them is measured on, any figures the client stated, the constraints they named such as budget, timing, and internal capacity, what they have already tried, and who has to approve. Where a needed input is missing, mark it in the deck as an open question rather than inventing a plausible version.

## The structure

**Our understanding.** Their situation, played back. Use their words, including their names for things. Three or four points, each one something they said, not something inferred. This is the section that earns the rest of the deck, so it goes first and it is written last, after the approach is settled.

**Solution hypothesis.** One paragraph, framed as a hypothesis and not a conclusion: given the situation, we believe the constraint is X, and addressing it would produce Y. Stating it as a hypothesis invites correction, which is what this meeting is for.

**Engagement objectives.** Three to five, each measurable, each tied to something in the understanding section. "Improve enablement" is not an objective. "Cut ramp time for a new hire from five months to three, measured by first closed deal" is.

**Approach and design principles.** How the work will run, and the two or three principles that govern the choices inside it. The principles matter more than the phases, because they are what the client will hold you to when something unexpected happens.

**Deliverables.** What they receive, in a form they can point to. A named artefact with a format and an owner, not an activity.

**Phases and milestones.** Sequence with duration and a milestone that is a verifiable event rather than a percentage. Show dependencies on the client's own inputs, because those are the ones that slip.

**Value proposition.** What changes if this works, sized where the client's own numbers allow it and left unsized where they do not. An invented figure here undoes the credibility the understanding section built.

**Next steps.** One primary ask with an owner and a date. A deck that ends with three asks ends with none.

## Writing rules

Every claim about the client traces to something in discovery. Every number is theirs or is marked as an assumption to confirm. Titles carry the message, so a slide titled "Approach" says less than "Fix the qualification gap first, then automate". One idea per slide. No feature lists. Nothing about your firm before the understanding section.

## Common mistakes

Leading with credentials. Objectives that cannot be measured. A phase plan with no client dependencies, which is a plan that will slip and surprise everyone. Deliverables described as activities. Sizing the value with a benchmark from another industry. A next step that asks for a decision nobody in the room can make.

## Quality bar

- The understanding section uses the client's own language and contains no inference presented as fact.
- Every objective is measurable and traceable to the understanding section.
- Milestones are verifiable events with dates, and client dependencies are visible.
- Every figure is the client's own or marked as an assumption to confirm.
- Exactly one next step, with an owner and a date.
