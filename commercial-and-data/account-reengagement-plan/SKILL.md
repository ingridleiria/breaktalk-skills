# Account Re-engagement Plan

Restarting a relationship is not selling. The buyer already has an opinion about the work, formed from an experience the seller may not have witnessed, and often held by a person who was not there. The most common failure is proposing the next phase before understanding why the last one stopped. This skill enforces the reverse order: diagnose, then rebuild the shared picture, then propose.

## Step 1: Build the dossier

Assemble what is known and, more importantly, mark what is not. Use connected tools for firmographics, contact changes, and news; fall back to web research. Never present an inference as a fact.

- The account: what the company does, its size and segment, recent news, funding, leadership changes, and anything that would have changed its priorities since the work paused.
- The engagement history: what was scoped, what was delivered, what outcomes were stated at the start, and what evidence exists that they were met.
- The stop: when activity ended, what the last recorded interaction was, and who was in the room.
- The people: who sponsored the work, who used it, who paid for it, and which of them are still there.
- The commercial position: value delivered to date, value of the paused scope, and any contractual position such as an unexpired master agreement.

Close the dossier with an explicit open questions list. Every item that is a hypothesis rather than a fact is labelled as one.

## Step 2: Diagnose why it stopped

Write the hypothesis before the outreach, and design the first conversation to test it. The causes behave differently:

| Cause | Signal | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Budget or priority shift | Work paused at a planning boundary, no complaint on record | Reframe against the new priority; a smaller first step usually clears |
| Sponsor departure | Champion left, activity stops within a quarter | Start from zero; the new person owes the old decision nothing |
| Adoption gap | Delivered, then unused | Root cause conversation first; more scope makes it worse |
| Unmet expectations | Outcome language differs between the two sides | Surface the specific gap before defending anything |
| Capacity | Client team absorbed by another programme | Timing question, not a value question; agree a revisit trigger |
| Delivery dissatisfaction | Escalations, tone change, silence after a specific event | Requires acknowledgement before anything else |

Assume the least self-flattering cause the evidence allows, and confirm it in conversation rather than assuming the pause was administrative.

## Step 3: Map the stakeholders as they are now

Rebuild the map rather than reusing the old one. For each person: role, relationship to the earlier work, what they are measured on, and what they would need to believe. Mark explicitly who is new, who inherited the relationship, and who has no memory of the original case. A person who inherited a paused project inherited someone else's decision, and is usually more open than the person who made it.

## Step 4: Sequence the conversations

1. **Reconnect.** Ask for a catch-up, not a meeting about the next phase. The stated purpose is to understand where things stand and to bring the new stakeholder up to speed.
2. **Diagnose.** Three questions do most of the work: what is your understanding of where this stands; what would have to be true for it to move; what has changed internally since we last worked together.
3. **Reconstruct the picture.** Restate the original objective, then update it out loud against what has changed. The point is a jointly owned current version, not a defence of the old one.
4. **Size the smallest credible next step.** Propose the ninety-day version that produces one visible result, not the full paused scope.
5. **Agree the trigger.** If it is genuinely not the time, leave with a named condition and a dated check-in rather than a vague revisit.

## Step 5: Write the objection playbook

Restart objections differ from cold objections and are answered with curiosity, not pressure. For each, record the type, the underlying question, the response, the goal, and the trap.

Cover at least: the new stakeholder who does not know the history; internal change and change fatigue; the results did not match expectations; budget priorities moved; wait until things settle; prove the earlier phases worked before we spend again; the team never adopted what was built; the scope is too broad to know where to start.

The two standing rules: do not defend prior delivery before understanding the specific concern, and treat a stall as a request for a smaller first step rather than a request for more persuasion.

## Step 6: Prepare the rehearsal

Produce two practice personas with an opening mood, the pains they actually feel, the objections they will raise, and one thing they will not volunteer until they trust the seller. Default pair: the inheriting stakeholder who is open but sceptical, and the executive who wants proof of prior return before approving more. Hand these to a role play session before the real conversation.

## Output

A single brief: dossier with open questions marked, the diagnosis and its evidence, the current stakeholder map, the conversation sequence with the actual questions, the objection playbook, and the two rehearsal personas.

## Quality bar

- Every fact in the dossier is sourced, and every hypothesis is labelled as one.
- A named diagnosis exists before any proposal appears in the plan.
- The stakeholder map reflects who is there now, not who was there then.
- The first ask is a conversation, and the first proposal is the smallest credible step.
- No claim about value delivered is made without evidence that survives the client repeating it back.
