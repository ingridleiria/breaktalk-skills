# Newsletter Post Writer

A newsletter earns its readers' Sunday morning by being about something specific, argued by someone specific. Generic essays about "the future of work" get skimmed; a post about a Tuesday conversation with one colleague and the paper that explains it gets forwarded. This skill writes the second kind.

## Before writing

Establish with the author:

1. **The one thing the post says.** One sentence. If it cannot be written, the post has two topics or none.
2. **The scene.** A concrete moment the post opens on: a conversation, a number that surprised, a mistake, a Sunday-morning observation. The scene must be real and must connect to the argument by the end of the first section.
3. **The lens.** The discipline or framework the author brings that a reader could not get elsewhere: economics, operations, a specific professional practice. The lens is stated by name and used to explain, not to decorate.
4. **The evidence.** Two to five sources the argument stands on: a paper, a dataset, a case, the author's own numbers. Verified before use; a newsletter that cites a study that does not exist loses its readers when one of them checks.
5. **The reader's takeaway.** What they can do or think differently on Monday.

Calibrate to the author's voice from three to five of their previous posts: sentence rhythm, paragraph length, how they open and close, what they never say. Keep a voice note for reuse. When a brand skill exists for the publication (for BreakTalk, the breaktalk-brand skill), its editorial rules apply.

## Structure

- **Opening** (150 to 300 words): the scene, in the first person, with sensory or numerical specifics. No thesis statement yet; the reader should want to know why this moment matters.
- **The turn** (one paragraph): the scene connects to the question the post answers.
- **The argument** (three to five sections with plain headers that state a point, not a topic): each section makes one claim, supports it with evidence named in the text (author, year, what they found, with a link), illustrates with a concrete example, and connects to the lens. Sections vary in length.
- **The counter-case** (one section or paragraph): the strongest objection, stated fairly and answered, or conceded with its consequence.
- **The close** (100 to 200 words): return to the opening scene with what it means now, state the takeaway in one or two sentences, end on the point. No summary of the post, no call to "stay tuned", no inspirational line.

Length by purpose: 1,200 to 1,800 words for a single-idea post, 2,500 to 3,500 for a researched essay. Longer is not better; the post is as long as the argument.

## Writing rules

- First person, present or past tense as the scene requires, consistent throughout.
- Specific over general in every sentence: names, numbers, places, dates, sources.
- Short and long sentences mixed; paragraphs of uneven length; no paragraph that ends by summarizing itself.
- No em dashes; commas, colons, or a new sentence instead.
- No stock vocabulary: delve, landscape, crucial, pivotal, leverage, seamless, transformative, navigate, unlock, journey, game-changer, at the end of the day.
- No rhetorical questions in series, no tricolons by reflex, no "it's not X, it's Y" constructions.
- Headers in the publication's convention; subscribe prompts placed where the platform requires, never mid-argument.
- Links on the source's name, not on "here" or "this study".

## Editing an existing draft

Diagnose first: mark the opening (scene or abstraction), the one thing (present or absent), each section's claim and evidence, the counter-case (present or absent), and the close (returns to the point or trails off). List the generic sentences and the stock vocabulary. Then rewrite with the diagnosis visible, so the author learns the pattern. Use the human-voice-editor skill for the final pass.

## Deliverables

The post in Markdown ready to paste into the platform, a title in the publication's convention (for colon titles: broad concept, colon, specific mechanism), a subtitle, a three-sentence preview for email and social, and the source list with links verified.

## Quality bar

- The post opens on a real moment and returns to it.
- Every claim has a named source or the author's own numbers.
- The lens is used, not mentioned.
- The last sentence is the point.
- A reader who knows the author would recognize the voice.
