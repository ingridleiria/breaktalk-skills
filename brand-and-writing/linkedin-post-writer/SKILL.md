# LinkedIn Post Writer

Most LinkedIn posts are written for the feed's mechanics rather than for a reader: a one-line hook, a story with a twist, a lesson in three bullets, a question to farm comments. Readers recognize the shape and skim past it. A post that states one specific idea, with one piece of evidence, in the author's real voice, reads as a person talking and gets read to the end. This skill writes that post.

## Before writing

- **The one idea.** One sentence. A post with two ideas is two posts.
- **The evidence.** A number, a case, an observation from the author's own work, a paper (verified). Without it, the post is an opinion, and the feed has enough.
- **The reason to post now.** A milestone, a publication, a conversation this week, a public event. Posts anchored in time read as real.
- **The author's voice**, from their previous posts or their newsletter: register, sentence length, whether they use "I" freely, what they never do.

## Shape

- **First line**: the idea or the concrete observation, stated plainly. Not a hook, not a question, not "Unpopular opinion:", not a one-word line for effect. The first line is what shows before "see more"; it should be a sentence the author would say aloud.
- **Body**: two to four short paragraphs. The evidence with its source, the reasoning in one or two moves, and what it implies. Full sentences and real paragraphs; the one-sentence-per-line format signals content built for scrolling rather than reading.
- **Close**: the point, stated once. Optionally one specific question if the author genuinely wants answers; never "Agree?" or "Thoughts?".
- **Link**: in the post body when the platform allows it without penalty for the author's audience; otherwise in the first comment, mentioned in the post. Name what the link is.
- **Length**: 120 to 300 words. Under 120 is a caption; over 300 is a newsletter.

## Types

- **Announcement** (a publication, a launch, a new role, a repository): what it is in the first sentence, why it exists in the second paragraph, what the reader gets from it, the link. Thanks to specific people by name if warranted, never a list of tags.
- **Observation**: something noticed at work this week, the number or fact behind it, what it means. The most readable type.
- **From the newsletter**: the single strongest idea from the issue, not a summary; one quote or number from the post; the link. The LinkedIn post should stand alone for people who will never click.
- **Reshare with commentary**: what the shared item says in one sentence, why the author disagrees or extends it in two or three, and the link.

## Rules

- No em dashes; no stock vocabulary (game-changer, thrilled, excited to announce, humbled, journey, unlock, leverage, thought leader).
- No hashtags beyond one or two that name a real topic, at the end.
- No tagging people who are not part of the story.
- No stories that are obviously constructed for a lesson; the reader's detector for these is well trained.
- No emojis as bullets; emojis at all only if the author uses them naturally.
- Numbers and names specific; "a client" is fine when confidentiality requires it, "a Fortune 500 company" is decoration.
- The post is true. A post that inflates a result is discovered by someone who was there.

## Reviewing a draft

Mark: the first line (statement or hook), the idea count, the evidence (present or absent), the format (paragraphs or broken lines), the stock vocabulary, the close (point or bait). Rewrite with the marks shown. Use the human-voice-editor skill for the final pass.

## Deliverables

The post text, the first-comment text if a link goes there, and a note on the best posting time for the author's audience if known.

## Quality bar

- One idea, one piece of evidence, one point at the end.
- First line is a plain sentence the author would say.
- Paragraphs, not broken lines.
- Nothing in it exists to game the feed.
- Under 300 words.
