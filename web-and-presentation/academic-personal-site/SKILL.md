# Academic Personal Site

Three people visit an academic site and they want different things. A search committee wants to know in ninety seconds what you work on and whether the record supports the claim. An editor or a referee wants a paper, quickly. A potential collaborator or a student wants to know whether your interests overlap theirs and how to reach you.

Serve all three from one page that loads instantly. Almost everything else people put on these sites serves none of them.

## Above the fold

Your name, your position and institution, and one or two sentences saying what you work on in words a colleague in an adjacent field would understand. Not a field label, an actual description: what question, in what setting, using what kind of evidence.

A photograph, because people at conferences want to recognise you. An email address, written so a person can use it. Links to a scholar profile, a persistent researcher identifier, a repository, and wherever your code lives.

That is the ninety seconds. Everything below is for people who decided to stay.

## Publications

The section people actually come for, so make it findable and citable.

Group by type, published first, then working papers, then work in progress, then other writing. Within each, reverse chronological. Full citation, all coauthors in the correct order, and a link that works: the DOI as canonical, plus a hosted version where the publisher's agreement permits it, clearly labelled as which version it is.

Give each substantial paper a one-line plain description of what it finds. A reader deciding whether to open a PDF is deciding on that line.

Keep the status of working papers accurate and dated. A paper listed as "under review" for three years is read as a paper that was rejected repeatedly and never updated, which may be unfair and is unavoidable. Update or move it.

Where a paper has a project page, link it. `research-paper-website` covers those.

## Working papers and work in progress

Distinguish them. A working paper is circulating and can be read; work in progress is a title and a sentence. Listing an unwritten paper as available is a small trap you set for yourself when someone asks for it.

Say what you would like to be contacted about. Most collaboration starts with someone reading a title and not knowing whether writing is welcome.

## Teaching, supervision, and service

What you teach and at what level. Whether you are taking students, which prospective students look for and almost nobody states. Editorial and refereeing service where the field values it.

Where you have teaching material worth sharing, share it. It is the cheapest way to be useful to people who will never cite you.

## The CV

One PDF, dated in the file name, matching the site exactly. A CV that lists a paper the site does not is the kind of inconsistency that gets noticed by exactly the people you least want to notice it.

Set a rhythm: update both together, twice a year and after anything material.

## Build and maintain

Static files on a domain you control. A university page will disappear when you move institutions, taking every link with it; use one if required, and make your own page the canonical one.

No build step you will have forgotten in two years. The most common failure of an academic site is not that it looks dated, it is that updating it became annoying, so it stopped being updated, and a stale site is worse than none because everything on it is now a claim about a past state.

Accessibility as standard: semantic headings, real alternative text, contrast that passes, and a page that reflows on a phone. Fast on a slow connection, since readers in much of the world are on one.

## What to leave out

A blog you will abandon after three posts. A landing animation. A hero image with no information. Anything requiring a login. A visitor counter. A publications list that is a screenshot. A contact form instead of an email address.

## Quality bar

- What you work on is stated in plain words above the fold, with a working email address.
- Every publication has a resolvable link, and hosted versions are labelled as which version they are.
- Working paper status is accurate and dated.
- Whether you are taking students is stated.
- The CV matches the site, and both are updated together.
- The site is on a domain you control, static, and has no build step.
- Headings are semantic, images have real alternative text, and the page works on a phone.
