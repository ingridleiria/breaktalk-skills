# Thesis Chapter Review

An examiner is not a proofreader and not a supporter. They are asking one question in several forms: does this text demonstrate that the candidate can conduct independent research to a standard the field accepts. This skill reads for that, and separates the findings by consequence, because a candidate who cannot tell a fatal problem from a preference will spend the last month fixing the wrong things.

Read the whole text before writing anything. Chapter-level problems are usually visible only against the rest of the thesis.

## What is examined

**The question.** Is there one, is it stated in a single sentence, and is it a question rather than a topic. A chapter about a subject cannot fail well or succeed well, because there is nothing to be right about.

**The answer.** Does the chapter answer the question it asked, in the conclusion, in words, without hedging into a summary of what was done. Where the answer is negative or partial, is it stated as a finding rather than buried.

**The design.** Does the method support the claim being made. This is where most theses are actually decided. An examiner will ask what would have to be true for the estimate to be causal, whether that has been argued or assumed, and what the most obvious rival explanation is and where it was addressed.

**The contribution.** Stated explicitly, and defensible against the literature actually cited. Being first in a setting is a contribution only if the setting changes something. If the contribution is that nobody has done this here, the examiner will ask why that matters, and the text must already answer.

**Internal consistency.** The question in the introduction, the hypotheses, the specifications estimated, the tables presented, and the claims in the conclusion must all be the same object. Drift between them is common, invisible to the author, and immediately visible to a reader coming to it fresh.

**Evidence to claim.** Every claim in the text traces to a number in a table or a figure. Every table and figure is referred to in the text and does work. Anything that appears in neither direction is cut.

**Limitations.** Stated by the author, before the examiner states them, and stated at the size they actually are. A limitations section that lists only sample size while ignoring the identification problem reads as either unaware or evasive, and both are worse than the limitation.

**Scholarly apparatus.** Citations that exist and say what they are cited for, notation defined at first use and used consistently, tables readable without the text, and a reference list that matches the citations.

## The report

Rank by consequence, not by page order.

- **Would fail or require major revision.** The claim the design cannot support, the question the chapter does not answer, the contribution that does not survive the literature, the result that contradicts another chapter. Each one with what is wrong, why it is fatal, and the smallest change that fixes it.
- **Required corrections.** Real problems with bounded fixes: a missing robustness check an examiner will ask for, an unsupported sentence, a table that does not show what the text claims, a limitation not acknowledged.
- **Preferences.** Say plainly that these are preferences, and say the author may ignore them. Mixing these in with the first category is the most common way a review does damage.

Then the questions. Ten to fifteen, in the words an examiner would use, ordered from the one most likely to be asked first. Include the two or three the text seems designed to avoid, because those are the ones that will be asked.

## Tone

Direct about the work, never about the person. No praise that is not specific, because generic encouragement makes the criticism unreadable. Where something is genuinely good, say what and why, since the author needs to know what to protect during revision.

## Quality bar

- Findings ranked by consequence, with the fatal ones separated from the fixable and the optional.
- Every finding points to a specific page, table, or sentence.
- The design section names the rival explanation and where the text does or does not address it.
- The viva questions are written in an examiner's words, ready to be answered.
- Preferences are labelled as preferences.
