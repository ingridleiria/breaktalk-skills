# Response to Reviewers

A revise-and-resubmit is an invitation with conditions. The response letter is the document that proves each condition was met or explains, with evidence, why it should not be. Editors read it before rereading the paper, and referees read it to decide whether to fight; its tone and completeness decide the outcome as much as the revision itself.

## Step 1: Triage the decision

Read the editor's letter and every report in full. Classify the decision (minor revision, major revision, reject and resubmit, or a rejection worth appealing) and extract the editor's own priorities, which override the referees' where they differ. Then decide whether to revise here or submit elsewhere: revise when the concerns are addressable and the venue is right; move on when the referees demand a different paper or the editor's letter signals low enthusiasm. State the recommendation with reasons.

## Step 2: Build the comment ledger

Number every distinct comment from every referee and the editor, one row each, even the small ones. Columns: source (R1, R2, editor), comment quoted in full, category (identification, estimation, data, literature, framing, writing, exhibit), severity (must address, should address, optional), and the initial disposition: comply, partially comply, or decline. Where two referees raise the same issue, link the rows and answer once, cross-referenced.

## Step 3: Decide each disposition honestly

- **Comply** when the referee is right or the change is cheap and harmless. Most comments land here. Do the work: rerun the analysis, add the exhibit, rewrite the section.
- **Partially comply** when the referee's concern is valid but the proposed remedy is wrong or infeasible. Address the concern by another route and explain why.
- **Decline** only with evidence: a citation the referee missed (verified), a robustness result showing the concern does not change the conclusion, a data limitation that makes the request impossible (stated plainly, and added to the limitations). Declining without evidence is the fastest route to rejection at the second round.

Never claim a change that was not made. Editors check.

## Step 4: Write the response letter

Structure:

1. **Opening** (one paragraph): thanks stated once and briefly, a summary of the main changes in three to five sentences, and a note on where the tracked-changes and clean versions are.
2. **Response to the editor**: each of the editor's points first.
3. **Response to each referee**, in order, each comment quoted verbatim in italics or a box, followed by the response, followed by the exact change made with the location (section, page, table number) and, where the change is short, the new text quoted.
4. **Summary of other changes** not requested by referees (updated data, corrected typos, added citations), listed briefly so nothing surprises the editor.

Tone rules: respectful, direct, never defensive or flattering. "We agree and have added..." or "We understand the concern; however, as Table A4 shows..." Address the referee's argument, never the referee. Do not apologize repeatedly, and do not thank the referee for every comment.

## Step 5: Deliver the revision

- A tracked-changes version and a clean version of the manuscript.
- New or changed exhibits regenerated by code, with the code updated in the replication package.
- Numbers changed anywhere propagated everywhere: abstract, introduction, results, discussion, tables.
- A final pass with the peer-review-simulator on the revised manuscript, because a revision often introduces new problems.

## Appeals

Only when the decision rests on a factual error by a referee or editor, and only with the evidence laid out in one page. Appeals over judgment calls fail and cost goodwill. If an appeal is warranted, write it as a short factual note to the editor, not an argument.

## Quality bar

- Every comment from every report appears in the ledger and the letter.
- Every "comply" corresponds to a verifiable change with a location.
- Every "decline" carries evidence.
- Numbers are consistent across the revised manuscript.
- The letter can be read by the editor in ten minutes and by each referee in five.
