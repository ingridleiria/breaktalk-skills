# Introduction Writer

An editor decides in the introduction whether to send the paper out for review. The introduction therefore has to do the whole paper's work in miniature: show a problem worth solving, show that it has not been solved, say what this paper does, say what it found in numbers, and say why that matters. Five moves, in that order, with nothing else.

## The five moves

1. **The problem in the world.** Open with the phenomenon, not the literature. A fact with a number and a source: the size of the program, the share of students affected, the amount of money at stake, the policy debate that turns on the answer. Two to four sentences. The reader should know by the end of the paragraph why anyone outside the subfield would care.
2. **The gap.** What is known, briefly and with verified citations, and what is not, precisely. The gap is not "little research exists"; it is "existing estimates come from cross-sectional comparisons that cannot separate selection from effect", or "no study observes the outcome after the reform in the population it targeted". Name the closest papers and say what they could not do.
3. **What this paper does.** The question, the setting, the data (source, units, years, sample size), and the design, in one paragraph. Written so the reader could describe the paper to a colleague from this paragraph alone.
4. **What it finds.** The main result with its magnitude in interpretable units, the key heterogeneity or mechanism if established, and the robustness in one clause. Numbers here, identical to the results section. An introduction that withholds the finding to build suspense is rejected for being unclear.
5. **The contribution.** Two or three specific contributions, each tied to a named literature with citations: the first estimate of X in setting Y; a design that addresses Z; evidence on a mechanism the literature assumed. Not "we contribute to the literature on education"; that is a topic, not a contribution.

Then, only where the field expects it (most economics journals do, most management journals do not), a one-paragraph roadmap of the sections.

## Length and shape

Two to four pages in a journal article, proportionally more in a thesis chapter. Paragraphs of uneven length; the problem paragraph is often short and the design paragraph long. No subsection headers inside the introduction.

## Diagnosing an existing introduction

Before rewriting, mark the draft move by move: label each paragraph with the move it performs (or "none"). Common findings, and their fixes:

- Opens with the literature instead of the world: move the phenomenon to the front.
- Gap stated as an absence rather than a limitation: rewrite the gap around what prior designs could not do.
- Finding absent or vague: insert the number from the results section.
- Contribution generic: replace with specific claims tied to named papers.
- Move 3 missing the data or the design: add them; editors need to see identification early.
- Paragraphs that summarize themselves at the end: cut the summary sentence.
- Roadmap present where the field does not want one, or absent where it does.

Show the diagnosis, then the rewrite.

## Writing rules

- Every empirical claim carries a verified citation, in the sentence that makes it, following the literature-verification skill. Nothing cited from memory.
- Numbers with sources and years. The introduction of an empirical paper is dense with them.
- Register: impersonal or first person plural according to the field and the venue; consistent throughout.
- Causal language calibrated to the design, from the first mention of the finding.
- No "this paper explores", "sheds light", "fills a gap", "to the best of our knowledge" (unless followed by a verifiable claim), "in recent years", "increasingly".
- End the introduction on the contribution or the roadmap, not on an uplift sentence.

## Coordination with the rest of the paper

The introduction is written after the results exist and revised whenever they change. The numbers in move 4 are copied from the results tables, not remembered. The contributions in move 5 match the discussion section's claims exactly. The abstract (see abstract-and-title) is the introduction compressed, and the two must agree.

## Quality bar

- The five moves are present, in order, and each can be pointed to.
- The main finding appears with a number by the end of the second page.
- Every citation is verified and specific.
- The problem paragraph would interest a reader outside the subfield.
- The contributions are claims a referee can check, not descriptions of the topic.
