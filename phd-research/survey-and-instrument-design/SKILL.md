# Survey and Instrument Design

A badly written question produces a number, and the number looks exactly like a good one. That is the whole problem: measurement error in a survey is invisible in the output and fatal to the conclusion, and no amount of estimation afterwards recovers it.

Design in this order: what you are measuring, how it has been measured before, then the questions, then the pilot. Writing questions first is the most common mistake and it produces an instrument nobody can interpret.

## Define the construct first

For every quantity in your hypotheses, write down what it is, at what level it exists, and what would count as more or less of it. "Job satisfaction" is not a construct until you have said whether you mean satisfaction with the role, the employer, or the career, and whether it is a state or a disposition.

Then map each construct to the items that will measure it, and check the reverse: every item should belong to a named construct. Items belonging to nothing are the ones that make the questionnaire long and the analysis vague.

## Use what exists

Prefer a validated instrument to a new one, even an imperfect one, for three reasons: its measurement properties are known, its results are comparable to other work, and referees do not have to take your word for it.

Where you adapt one, say exactly what you changed and why, and expect to have to defend it. Where no validated instrument exists, say so, and treat your new measure as something to be validated rather than assumed.

## Writing the questions

One idea per question. "Was the service fast and friendly" cannot be answered by someone who found it fast and rude, and the answer they give is noise.

Neutral wording. A question that signals the expected answer will get it. Ask "how would you describe" rather than "how satisfied were you with", when satisfaction is what you are trying to measure rather than assume.

Concrete over abstract. "How many times in the last thirty days" beats "how often, generally", because the second is answered by a self-image rather than by a memory.

Match the reference period to what people can actually recall. Beyond about a month, most recall is reconstruction, and for frequent events it is reconstruction of a rate rather than a count.

Watch for social desirability on anything about income, effort, prejudice, health behaviour, or compliance. Where it matters, use indirect techniques, guarantee and describe the anonymity, or accept that the level is biased and only the variation is usable.

Avoid negations, especially double ones, and avoid jargon the respondent would not use unprompted.

## Response scales

Choose deliberately and use the same choice throughout. Points on the scale, whether there is a midpoint, whether the labels are all present or only at the ends, and whether "do not know" and "not applicable" are offered are all decisions that change the distribution you get.

Offer "do not know" where not knowing is genuinely possible, or you will collect guesses. Do not offer it as an easy exit on questions everyone can answer.

Be careful with agreement scales, which are prone to people agreeing with whatever is offered. Where you use them, include reverse-worded items and check that they behave as expected in the pilot; if they do not, the respondents were not reading, which is itself a finding about your instrument.

Treat ordinal scales as ordinal in the analysis unless you have a reason not to, and state the choice.

## Order, routing, and length

Early questions shape later ones. Put easy and non-threatening items first, sensitive ones late, and demographics at the end unless they route the questionnaire. Where order could affect the results, randomise blocks and record the order each respondent saw.

Keep it short. Beyond roughly fifteen minutes, data quality falls: straight-lining, dropout, and hurried answers. Every item should earn its place by being in the analysis plan; if you cannot say which hypothesis an item serves, cut it.

## Translation

Back-translation as the minimum: translate, have a second independent translator return it to the original, and reconcile the differences. Then have someone bilingual and domain-familiar review it for meaning rather than words. Concepts that do not exist in the target culture need adaptation and disclosure, not literal translation.

Pilot every language version separately. A translated instrument is a new instrument.

## Piloting

Not optional, and not a spell check. Run cognitive interviews with five to ten people from the actual population: have them answer aloud, saying what they think each question means and how they arrived at their answer. This finds the misreadings that no amount of desk review does.

Then a small live pilot to check timing, routing, dropout points, and the distribution of answers. An item where everyone answers the same way carries no information and should be cut or rewritten.

## Sampling and non-response

Define the population, the frame, and how units are selected, and be honest about the difference between the frame and the population.

Plan for non-response before it happens: the expected rate, how many reminders and when, and what you will do to assess whether respondents differ from non-respondents. Compare respondents to known population characteristics where possible, and report it. A high response rate is not required for a good study; an unexamined low one is a problem.

## Quality bar

- Every construct is defined before any question is written, and every item belongs to one.
- Validated instruments are used where they exist, and adaptations are documented.
- No double-barrelled, leading, or negated questions survive the review.
- Response scales are chosen deliberately and used consistently.
- Cognitive interviews were run with the actual population before fielding.
- Translations are back-translated and piloted separately.
- The non-response plan exists before fielding, and non-response is assessed afterwards.
- Every item maps to something in the analysis plan.
