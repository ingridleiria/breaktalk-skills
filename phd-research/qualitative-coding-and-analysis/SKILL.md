# Qualitative Coding and Analysis

Qualitative work is dismissed when it looks like the author read some interviews and reported what struck them. The remedy is not to pretend it is quantitative. It is to make every step visible: how the codes were arrived at, how consistently they were applied, how themes were built from codes, and why these quotations rather than others.

## Decide the approach and say so

**Inductive**, where codes emerge from the material, suits an area without established theory and produces categories nobody imposed. **Deductive**, where codes come from theory or a framework, suits testing whether an existing account holds here. **Hybrid**, a deductive frame with room for emergent codes, is the most common in practice and is legitimate when stated.

Also state the level: are you describing what participants said, or interpreting what it means. Both are valid; conflating them is what makes a section feel slippery.

## Build the codebook

Not a list of words. For each code: a name, a definition, an inclusion rule, an exclusion rule, and an example from the data. The exclusion rule is what makes a codebook usable by a second person, and writing it forces the boundary to be decided rather than felt.

Build it on a subset, three to five transcripts, then apply it to everything. Expect it to change; version it, date the versions, and record what changed and why. When a code changes materially, re-code the earlier material rather than leaving two standards in the same dataset.

Keep the codebook flat enough to use. Sixty codes across three levels is usually a sign that coding has become description.

## Code

Code the whole corpus, not the parts that look interesting, or you will find what you expected.

Where the claim depends on how often something appears, double code: two people code the same material independently, and disagreement is measured and reported with a statistic appropriate to the design rather than as a percentage. Percentage agreement overstates consistency because it ignores agreement by chance.

Resolve disagreements by discussion and record what the discussion changed. Disagreement that keeps recurring on one code means the definition is wrong, not that a coder is.

Where single coding is unavoidable, say so, and compensate: an audit of a sample by a second reader, or a documented reflexive account of what the coder brought to the reading.

## Build themes from codes

A theme is not a code with a longer name. It is a pattern that holds across the material and does work in the argument. Build it by grouping codes, checking the grouping against the raw material, and asking what the theme actually claims.

Then test it. Look specifically for cases that contradict it, because a theme that has never been challenged is an impression. Report negative cases and what they do to the claim: they may bound it, refine it, or break it, and all three are results.

Say how prevalent a theme is, in terms the data supports. "Most participants" is a claim about counts and needs the counts. "Several participants described" is honest where a count would be misleading given the sample.

## Quotations

Choose by a stated rule, not by eloquence. Typical rules: the clearest statement of a common position; the case that shows the boundary of a theme; a negative case. Say which rule you used.

Attribute consistently, with pseudonymous identifiers and whatever characteristics matter for interpretation. Quote enough context that the meaning is not created by the cropping. Mark every edit, including removed hesitation, with an ellipsis or a bracket, and never edit for fluency in a way that changes register.

Do not let one articulate participant carry an argument. Check how many of your quotations come from how few people.

## Saturation and sample

If you claim saturation, say how you judged it: at what point new material stopped producing new codes, and how much material you collected after that point to check. Saturation asserted without evidence is a convention rather than a finding.

Where the sample is small or purposive, describe it as it is and state what it can and cannot support. Qualitative work does not need to be representative; it needs to be honest about what it is.

## The audit trail

By the end there should be a path from any claim in the paper back to the coded extracts, back to the transcripts. Keep the codebook versions, the coding decisions, the agreement statistics, the analytic memos written along the way, and the notes on how themes were formed.

Where quotations can be shared without identifying anyone, share them. Where they cannot, say why, and preserve the trail internally.

## Mixed methods

State whether the qualitative work generates hypotheses that the quantitative work tests, explains a quantitative result, or stands alongside it as a separate strand. Say what happens when the two disagree, in advance, because they will and the temptation afterwards is to report only the agreement.

## Quality bar

- The approach and the level of interpretation are stated.
- The codebook has definitions with inclusion and exclusion rules and examples, and is versioned.
- The whole corpus is coded, not a selection.
- Agreement is measured with an appropriate statistic where prevalence claims are made, or single coding is disclosed and compensated.
- Negative cases were sought and reported.
- Quotations are selected by a stated rule, and no participant carries an argument alone.
- Prevalence claims match what the data can support.
- An audit trail runs from claim to extract to transcript.
