# Abstract and Title

The abstract is read a hundred times more often than the paper, and the title a thousand times more often than the abstract. Both are decided by the same rule: they state what the paper found, with the numbers, in the fewest words that remain precise.

## The abstract, sentence by sentence

Build it from the finished results, never from intentions. Each sentence has a job; each claim traces to a specific exhibit.

1. **The question and why it matters** (one sentence, sometimes two): what is asked and the setting. No throat-clearing about the importance of the topic in general.
2. **The data and the design** (one or two sentences): the source, the units, the years, the sample size, and the identification strategy named. "Using administrative data on 142 institutions from 2009 to 2019 and a staggered difference-in-differences design..."
3. **The main finding with its magnitude** (one or two sentences): direction, size in interpretable units, precision where it matters. "Treatment raises the outcome by 0.12 standard deviations" is an abstract sentence; "we find significant effects" is not.
4. **The mechanism or heterogeneity** (one sentence), only if the paper establishes it.
5. **The contribution or implication** (one sentence), sized to the evidence, ending on the finding rather than on a call for future research.

Length: 150 to 250 words for most journals, one paragraph; check the target's limit and write to it. Structured abstracts (Purpose, Design, Findings, Originality) follow the same content rules under the required headings.

## Abstract rules

- Every number in the abstract appears in a table or figure in the paper, identically.
- No citations in the abstract unless the venue requires them.
- No undefined abbreviations.
- Causal verbs only if the design supports them; otherwise "is associated with".
- No "this paper explores", "sheds light on", "fills a gap", "novel", "comprehensive", "robust" (outside statistics), "nuanced".
- Written last, from the results, and revised whenever a number in the paper changes.

## Diagnosing a weak abstract

Read the draft and mark each sentence with its job from the list above. Sentences with no job are cut. Missing jobs are added. Then check: does the abstract contain the main number? Does it name the design? Does it state the sample and years? Does it end on the finding? Rewrite only after the diagnosis is shown.

## Titles

Generate eight to twelve candidates across formats, then rank:

- **Declarative** (states the finding): "Student loans raise graduation rates but not learning: evidence from Brazil".
- **Descriptive** (states the object): "The effect of student financing on learning outcomes in Brazilian higher education".
- **Question**: "Does market consolidation lower course quality?"
- **Colon form**: broad concept, colon, specific mechanism and population. This is the most common form in the social sciences and the safest.
- **Short phrase**, for working papers and talks.

Ranking criteria: does the reader know what the paper found or asked; are the population and setting named; is it under fifteen words; would it be found by a search on the paper's key terms; does it avoid puns that age badly. Check for collisions with existing titles by searching the candidates; a title identical to a published paper is rejected.

## Keywords, JEL codes, highlights

- Keywords: five to seven, drawn from the field's vocabulary and the journal's recent keyword lists, covering the topic, the method, and the setting.
- JEL codes: two to four, verified against the current JEL classification, matching the paper's topic and method.
- Highlights (where required): three to five bullet points under 85 characters each, each a finding with a number, not a description of the paper.

## Second-language versions

When a resumo or resumen is required, translate the finished abstract preserving the numbers and the structure, then verify the technical terms against the field's usage in that language rather than translating literally.

## Quality bar

- Each abstract sentence has a named job and every number traces to an exhibit.
- The design and the sample are named.
- The title states the finding or the question and names the setting.
- Keywords and JEL codes are verified against current lists.
