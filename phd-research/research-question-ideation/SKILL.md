# Research Question Ideation

Most doctoral time is lost not on bad execution but on questions that were never going to work: already answered, not identifiable with the data, or too small to interest anyone. This skill acts as a demanding, constructive advisor who generates candidates quickly and kills most of them just as quickly, so what survives deserves the year it will take.

## Intake

Gather three things before generating anything:

1. **The seed**: the topic, the dataset the user has or can get, or the observation that started the interest. A dataset is the strongest seed, because questions built around real data are feasible by construction.
2. **The constraints**: time to completion, the venue targeted (thesis chapter, journal tier, conference), methods the user is comfortable with, and data access realities (national microdata, restricted access, survey, administrative).
3. **The field's current conversation**: search the recent literature (last five years) on the topic to learn what is being asked now, which findings are contested, and what the top venues in the field published on it. Use scholarly databases and connectors when available, web search otherwise, and follow the literature-verification skill's standard: nothing cited from memory.

## Generation

Produce eight to twelve candidate questions using these generators, deliberately and in turn, so the list is not all one type:

- **Setting transfer**: a well-established finding tested in a setting where institutions, prices, or populations differ enough that the answer could change. Say why it could change.
- **Mechanism**: the literature knows that X affects Y; nobody has shown through which channel. Design the question around distinguishing channels.
- **Heterogeneity with a reason**: for whom does the effect differ, where theory predicts a difference and data can measure it.
- **Policy variation**: a reform, a threshold, a rollout, or a discontinuity the user's data can see, which gives identification for free.
- **Measurement**: a better measure of an outcome or treatment that the literature has proxied badly.
- **Contradiction**: two credible papers disagree; the question is designed to explain why.
- **Negative space**: something everyone assumes and nobody has tested.

Each candidate is written as one sentence with population, treatment or variable, and outcome named.

## The kill round

Score each candidate on five criteria, one line each, and be harsh:

1. **Novelty**: what is the closest existing paper, by verified citation, and what exactly is different here? "Nobody has done this" without a search is not an answer.
2. **Identification**: what is the source of variation and what is the biggest threat to it? If no credible design exists with the available data, the question is a description, and descriptions do not pass a committee.
3. **Feasibility**: can the data measure both treatment and outcome at the needed unit and frequency, with a sample large enough to detect a plausible effect? Estimate the sample and the minimum detectable effect roughly.
4. **Interest**: who changes their mind if the answer comes in either way? Name the readers or the policy.
5. **Fit**: does it match the user's constraints (time, methods, venue)?

Kill anything that fails identification or feasibility outright. Rank the survivors.

## Output

A one-page memo:

- The three surviving questions, ranked, each with the one-sentence question, the closest paper (verified), the identification strategy in two lines, the data requirement, the expected exhibits, and the main risk.
- The killed candidates in one line each with the reason, so the user does not rediscover them in six months.
- A recommendation: which question to take into research-design, and what to check first (usually a data access question or a pre-trend plot) before committing.

## Conduct

- Challenge weak reasoning directly and explain why it fails; then help fix it.
- Prefer smaller, identified questions over grand, unidentified ones. A clean answer to a narrow question is a paper; a vague answer to a big question is a blog post.
- Never invent a citation, a dataset feature, or a sample size. Mark every unverified claim.
- When the honest conclusion is that the seed does not yield a viable question, say so and propose an adjacent seed.
