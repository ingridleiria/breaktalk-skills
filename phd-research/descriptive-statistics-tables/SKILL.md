# Descriptive Statistics Tables

Descriptive tables are where a referee first decides whether to trust the paper. They show the sample is what the text claims, the treatment and comparison groups are comparable or visibly not, and the outcome varies enough to be studied. This skill builds them to be read, not skipped.

## The three standard exhibits

### 1. Sample construction table

Every restriction from raw data to analysis sample, in order, with the observation count after each step and the count dropped. This table often goes in the data section or the appendix, and it is the exhibit that answers "where did the sample come from". Generate it from the cleaning code, not by hand, using the data-profiling-and-cleaning skill's log.

### 2. Summary statistics (Table 1)

Variables in rows, grouped under headers (outcomes, treatment, covariates, institutional characteristics), with mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and N, for the analysis sample. Rules:

- Units in the variable label or in a note, always.
- Continuous and binary variables reported appropriately: for binaries, the mean is a share, and minimum and maximum add nothing, so drop them or note it.
- Skewed variables (revenue, size) reported with the median alongside the mean, or in logs if that is how they enter the model.
- The sample of Table 1 is the sample of the main regression. If they differ, say why and by how much.
- Variable order follows the order in which the paper introduces them.
- No more than about 25 rows; secondary variables go to an appendix table.

### 3. Balance table

Means by group (treated versus comparison, or above versus below the cutoff, or by cohort), the difference, and a test of the difference, with standard errors clustered as in the main model when appropriate. Rules:

- Report normalized differences as well as p-values for large samples, where every difference is significant and the p-value says little.
- Imbalance is reported, not hidden. Where covariates differ, the text says so and explains how the design handles it (fixed effects, controls, matching, or the difference being irrelevant to the identifying assumption).
- For DiD, balance in pre-period levels is informative but not required; balance in pre-period trends is the relevant check, and it lives in the identification exhibit, not here.
- For RDD, balance is at the cutoff, reported at the bandwidth used.

## Formatting

Follow the academic-tables-booktabs skill in this library: three horizontal rules, no vertical lines, right-aligned numbers, consistent decimals per column (usually two, three for shares near zero), standard errors or standard deviations in parentheses below or beside, and notes that define every abbreviation, the sample, the weights, and the source.

## The prose

Descriptive statistics are reported in the text, not just displayed. Three to six sentences after each table:

- The sample in plain terms: how many units, over which years, from which source.
- The two or three descriptive facts that matter for the story: the level of the outcome, how much it varies, how treated units differ from comparison units and whether that difference is a threat.
- Anything the reader would otherwise ask: why N differs across variables, why a value looks extreme.

State the numbers in the prose with the same precision as the table and name the table.

## Generation

Produce the tables from stored results, never by transcribing output: in Stata, estpost and esttab (or equivalent), exported to the paper's format; in Python, a documented script. The code that produced each table is saved alongside it, so a change in sample regenerates the table without hand edits.

## Quality bar

- The Table 1 sample matches the main estimation sample, or the difference is explained.
- Units and definitions appear for every variable.
- Imbalance, where present, is stated in the text with its consequence for the design.
- Every table has a note stating sample, source, and definitions.
- The tables are regenerated by code, not edited by hand.
