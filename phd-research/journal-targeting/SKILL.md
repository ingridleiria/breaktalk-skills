# Journal Targeting

Choosing the journal is a decision with a cost measured in months. The wrong target costs a desk rejection and a quarter; the right one costs a revision. This skill builds the ladder from evidence about each journal, not from reputation alone, and then reshapes the paper for the top rung.

## Step 1: Characterize the manuscript

Five lines: the question and field, the setting and data, the design and its credibility, the main finding and its magnitude, and the contribution as stated in the introduction. Add the user's constraints: indexing requirements (many programs require SSCI or Scopus for graduation), timeline, open access budget, and any journal already ruled out.

## Step 2: Build the candidate list

Ten to fifteen candidates from three sources: journals cited in the manuscript's own reference list (the strongest signal of fit), journals that published the closest papers found in the literature search, and the field's standard tiers. Search rather than recall; journal scopes and editorial boards change.

## Step 3: Evidence per journal

For each candidate, gather and record with sources and dates:

- **Scope fit**: the journal's stated aims, and three to five recent articles (last two years) close to the manuscript in topic, method, or setting. If no recent article is close, fit is weak regardless of prestige.
- **Indexing and rank**: confirmed against the relevant lists (Web of Science Master Journal List for SSCI, Scopus source list, JCR quartile, SJR quartile, ABDC, ABS, Qualis where relevant). Do not rely on the journal's own claim.
- **Metrics with the year**: impact factor or CiteScore, acceptance rate where published, time to first decision where published or reported by authors, article processing charges and open access model.
- **Editorial signals**: editors' fields, special issues open, recent editorials on what the journal wants.
- **Predatory screening**: publisher reputation, membership in COPE or OASPA, presence on recognized watchlists, unsolicited invitations, guaranteed fast acceptance, vague peer review, fees revealed late, editorial board unverifiable. Any of these disqualifies the venue, and the report says so plainly.

## Step 4: The ladder

Rank into a submission ladder of three to five journals: the reach target where the paper is a plausible fit at the top of its range, the match targets where fit and quality align, and the safe target that meets the constraints. For each: fit rationale in two lines, expected time to decision, likely revision demands, and the reframing needed. State the total expected time across the ladder under a realistic sequence of outcomes.

## Step 5: Tailor to the chosen target

Once a target is chosen, rework the manuscript to it:

- **Framing**: the introduction's problem and contribution stated in the terms the journal's recent articles use; the literature engaged is the literature the journal publishes.
- **Structure and length**: section names, word limits, abstract format (structured or not), number of tables and figures, appendix rules, all from the author guidelines fetched live.
- **Citations**: cite relevant recent work from the target journal where it genuinely bears on the argument (never as padding), and use its reference style.
- **Language and register**: economics journals want magnitudes and identification; education journals want implications for practice; management journals want theoretical contribution stated first.
- **Cover letter**: written to this journal's editor, naming the fit specifically (see the submission kit conventions).

Produce a checklist of every change made and every guideline rule complied with.

## After a rejection

Read the decision letter for the reason category (scope, contribution, identification, execution, writing). Scope and contribution rejections move the paper down the ladder without major revision; identification and execution rejections require revision before any resubmission, and the next target should be chosen only after the revision plan exists. Never submit the same manuscript to the next journal unchanged when substantive criticism was given.

## Conduct

- Every indexing and metric claim carries its source and the year checked.
- Prestige is not fit. A well-cited journal that has not published on the topic in years is a poor target.
- Predatory or questionable venues are named as such without hedging.
- Graduation or funding indexing requirements are treated as hard constraints.

## Quality bar

- The ladder has three to five journals with evidence per journal and a total expected timeline.
- Indexing verified against the official lists.
- Tailoring changes are listed and comply with fetched guidelines.
- The cover letter names the specific fit.
