# Research Proposal and Grant

A proposal is evaluated against criteria the reader has in front of them, by someone reading forty others. It wins when it answers those criteria explicitly, in the order the reader expects, with a design that a skeptic would believe can deliver the result on the timeline and budget stated. This skill builds it from the call outward.

## Step 1: Read the call as a rubric

Fetch or take the call, template, or program description and extract the evaluation criteria, the required sections, the length limits, the eligibility rules, the deadline, and the reviewers' likely profile (disciplinary committee, interdisciplinary panel, program officers, a university committee). Build a compliance checklist before writing. Proposals fail on missing sections more often than on weak science.

## Step 2: The core, from research-design

Bring the design document from the research-design skill (or build it first if it does not exist): the question, the hypotheses, the identification strategy, the model, the data. The proposal is that document expanded and argued for a reader who has not seen the data.

## Step 3: Standard proposal structure

Adapt the headings to the call's template; keep the content:

1. **Summary or abstract** (the length required): the question, the setting, the design, the expected contribution, and why this team or candidate. Written last.
2. **Motivation and significance**: the problem in the world with numbers and sources, and why answering it matters to the funder's stated goals. Map explicitly to the call's priorities where they exist.
3. **State of knowledge and gap**: the closest work with verified citations (literature-verification standard), framed as what prior designs could not establish. Short; reviewers know the literature.
4. **Research questions and hypotheses**: stated formally, each hypothesis falsifiable and tied to an expected exhibit.
5. **Design and methods**: identification in words, then the estimating approach, the assumption, the threats and the planned diagnostics, the power or minimum detectable effect estimate where feasible. A reviewer should be able to predict the paper's Table 2 from this section.
6. **Data and access**: sources, years, units, the access route and its status (obtained, requested, agreement pending), and the fallback if access fails. Access risk is the most common reason a funded project stalls; treat it seriously.
7. **Work plan and timeline**: phases with milestones and dates, in a table or a simple Gantt, realistic about data acquisition and cleaning (which usually take longer than analysis). Deliverables per phase.
8. **Expected outputs and dissemination**: papers with target venues, datasets or code to be released, policy briefs, presentations. Match to what the funder rewards.
9. **Budget and justification** (where required): each line tied to an activity in the work plan, with unit costs and the reason; personnel effort in months; data costs; travel; open access charges. No round numbers without a basis.
10. **Ethics, data management, and risk**: human subjects approval status, data protection for microdata, a data management plan, and the top three risks with mitigations.
11. **Candidate or team qualifications**: two paragraphs on the specific capabilities the design needs (methods, data experience, setting knowledge), not a CV summary.
12. **References**: verified, in the required style.

## Fellowship and scholarship variants

Research statements for fellowships and PhD applications are shorter (two to five pages) and personal in register: the question, why it matters, why the candidate is positioned to answer it, and what the fellowship makes possible. Keep the design content; drop the budget detail unless asked. Program-specific criteria (fit with a host institution, contribution to a field the program funds) are answered explicitly.

## Writing rules

- Every criterion in the call is answered where the reviewer will look for it, using the call's own vocabulary once so the match is visible.
- Numbers, sources, years throughout; no "important", "innovative", "significant" without evidence.
- Feasibility is argued, not asserted: data in hand or a named access route, methods the candidate has used, a timeline with slack.
- Limitations and risks stated by the author, with mitigations.
- Length limits respected exactly; over-length proposals are returned unread by many funders.

## Deliverables

The proposal in the required format, the compliance checklist with every item ticked, the budget spreadsheet where applicable (financial-model-builder conventions), and a one-paragraph plain summary for a non-specialist reader.

## Quality bar

- Every required section present and within limits.
- The design is specific enough that a reviewer can predict the main table.
- Data access status is stated honestly with a fallback.
- The timeline and budget reconcile with the work plan.
- All citations verified.
