# Refereeing for a Journal

This is the other side of peer review, and it is a skill in its own right. The report you write is read by an editor deciding, an author revising, and often a second referee calibrating. A report that is vague costs the author a revision cycle. A report that is cruel costs them more than that and teaches nobody anything.

For the reverse direction, simulating a review of your own paper before submitting it, use `peer-review-simulator`.

## Before accepting

Decline promptly rather than slowly. A review you will not complete is worse than a decline, because the editor loses weeks.

Accept if the paper is genuinely inside your competence, you have the time in the window offered, and you have no conflict. Decline, and say why in one line, if the paper is outside your area, if you cannot meet the deadline, or if a conflict exists. Where you are close but not expert, you can accept for the part you can judge and tell the editor explicitly which part that is, which is more useful to them than a confident review of something you cannot assess.

**Conflicts.** A current or recent co-author, a supervisor or student relationship, the same department, a personal relationship, a competing paper of your own in progress on the same question, or a financial interest. When in doubt, disclose to the editor and let them decide. Anonymity is not a reason to review something you should not.

If you recognise the authors, say so to the editor and continue only if the review can still be fair.

## Reading

Read the abstract and the conclusion first, then the tables, then the method, then the introduction. That order tells you quickly whether the claims are supported, which is the only question that decides the recommendation. Read the whole paper once before writing a word.

Then ask, in order: is the question worth answering, is it new, does the design answer it, do the results support the claims made, and would a reader be able to reproduce this. A paper can fail at any of these, and the earlier it fails the shorter your report should be.

## What a referee is for

You are judging whether the paper's claims are supported and whether the contribution is real. You are not rewriting the paper into the one you would have written, not requiring your own work to be cited, not demanding analysis that would be a different paper, and not enforcing your preferences about style, software, or estimator when the author's choice is defensible.

The most common failure in refereeing is scope creep: a list of additional analyses, each individually reasonable, which together amount to two more years of work. Before you request something, ask what it would change. If a negative result would not alter the paper's conclusion, it belongs in a suggestion, not a requirement.

## The report

**Summary of the paper in your own words**, three to five sentences: the question, the data, the design, the finding, the claimed contribution. This is not filler. It shows the author whether they were understood, and an author who was misread has learned something important about their writing.

**Assessment**, one paragraph: is the question important, is the contribution real, and does the evidence support the claim. State your overall view here rather than leaving the editor to infer it from the length of your list.

**Major points**, numbered, each one something that must be addressed for the paper to be publishable. For each: what the problem is, why it matters for the claim, and what would resolve it. Be specific enough to be actionable. "The identification is unconvincing" is not a referee comment; naming the assumption, why it is doubtful here, and what evidence would support it is.

**Minor points**, numbered, clearly separated: unclear sentences, missing definitions, table formatting, a citation needed, an inconsistency between numbers in the text and the table. Keep these genuinely minor. Padding this section to look thorough wastes everyone's time.

**Confidential comments to the editor.** Your recommendation and anything you cannot say to the author: a suspicion of overlap with published work, a conflict you noticed while reading, doubt about whether you are the right referee, or a judgement about fit with the journal. Do not contradict your report here, because editors notice, and a report that says one thing to the author and another to the editor is the least useful kind.

## Calibrating the recommendation

Match the recommendation to the report. A list of eight major points is not a minor revision, and one clarifying question is not a rejection.

Reject when the question is not answerable with this design or the contribution is not there, and say which, because the author needs to know whether to fix the paper or to abandon it. Major revision when the claims exceed the evidence but the evidence could be brought up to the claims, or the claims brought down to the evidence. Minor revision when the paper is sound and the issues are presentational or bounded. Accept as is happens rarely and is worth saying plainly when it does.

Recommend rejection quickly and kindly rather than slowly and thoroughly. A twelve-page report attached to a rejection reads as contempt.

## Tone

Write about the paper, never about the authors. Avoid sarcasm entirely; it survives anonymity badly and it is remembered. Where something is well done, say so specifically, because it tells the author what to keep. Assume competence and good faith, including when you are certain the paper is wrong.

Write as though your name were on it, because one day the field may be small enough that it effectively is.

## Quality bar

- The invitation answered promptly, and any conflict disclosed rather than reasoned away.
- The summary written in your own words, before any criticism.
- Major and minor points separated, and the majors genuinely major.
- Every requested change comes with what it would change about the conclusion.
- The recommendation matches the severity of the report, and the confidential comments do not contradict it.
- Nothing in the report addresses the authors rather than the work.
