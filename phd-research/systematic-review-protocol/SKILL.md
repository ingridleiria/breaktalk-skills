# Systematic Review Protocol

The difference between a systematic review and a literature review is that the systematic one can be repeated by somebody else and produce the same set of papers. Everything below exists to make that true. It is more work than it looks and most of the work happens before any paper is read.

For an evidence base assembled while writing rather than as a standalone review, use `literature-verification`.

## Write and register the protocol first

Before searching. It states the question, the criteria, the databases, the search strategy, the screening process, what will be extracted, how bias will be assessed, and how the results will be synthesised.

Register it publicly where the field has a registry. The point is that decisions about inclusion are made before you know which papers they include, because reviewers who choose criteria after seeing results choose the results.

Any deviation from the protocol is reported with its reason and the date.

## Frame the question so it is answerable

Use a structure appropriate to the field: population, intervention or exposure, comparator, outcome, and where relevant the study designs and context admitted. For scoping reviews, population, concept, and context.

The question determines the criteria and the criteria determine the search. A question that cannot be turned into inclusion criteria is a topic, and a review of a topic is a narrative review, which is a different and also legitimate thing that should be called by its name.

## Criteria, written before searching

Inclusion and exclusion, each specific enough to apply without judgement calls: population, exposure or intervention, comparator, outcomes reported, study design, time period, language, and publication status including whether unpublished work and preprints are eligible.

Every criterion needs a reason. Language and date restrictions in particular are asked about by reviewers, and "convenience" is an honest answer where it is the true one.

## The search

At least two or three databases appropriate to the field, plus the grey literature route if unpublished work is eligible, plus hand searching of the reference lists of included studies and of key journals.

Record for each database: the platform, the exact string including field tags and truncation, the date run, and the number of records returned. A search that cannot be rerun exactly is not a systematic search. Get the string reviewed by someone who builds them, ideally a librarian, since a badly built string is the most common invisible failure in these reviews.

Record the deduplication method and the number removed.

## Screening

Two people, independently, at title and abstract, then at full text. Report agreement and how disagreements were resolved. Where a single screener is unavoidable, say so and have a proportion checked by a second.

Record the reason for every full-text exclusion, since those are reported individually.

Report the counts as a flow: records identified per source, after duplicates removed, screened, full texts assessed, excluded with reasons, and included. Those numbers must add up, and a reviewer will check that they do.

## Extraction

Use a form defined in advance and piloted on a few studies. Extract at minimum: citation, country and setting, design, sample and its characteristics, exposure or intervention, comparator, outcomes and how they were measured, effect estimates with their uncertainty, the model and covariates, and funding and conflicts.

Two extractors where feasible, or one extracting and one checking. Contact authors for missing data and record whether they replied.

## Risk of bias

Assess every included study with a named tool appropriate to the designs included, and report the judgement per domain per study rather than a single score. Say who assessed and how disagreements were resolved.

Then use it. An assessment that appears in a table and never affects the synthesis is decoration. At minimum, check whether the result changes when high-risk studies are excluded.

## Synthesis

Decide whether the studies can be combined at all. Pooling estimates from studies with different populations, exposures, or outcome definitions produces a number with no referent, and doing it anyway is the most common serious error in this literature.

Where meta-analysis is appropriate: state the effect measure, the model and why, how heterogeneity is quantified and interpreted, any subgroup or meta-regression specified in the protocol, and the assessment of small-study effects if there are enough studies to support one.

Where it is not appropriate, synthesise structurally rather than narratively: group by design or population, tabulate effects with their uncertainty, describe direction and consistency, and say plainly where the evidence is too heterogeneous to support a pooled claim.

Assess the certainty of the body of evidence per outcome with a recognised framework, and report it.

## Reporting

Follow the reporting guideline for the review type and include its checklist. Report the protocol registration and any deviation. Report the search strings in full in an appendix. Report the excluded full texts with reasons. State funding and conflicts.

## Quality bar

- The protocol was registered before searching, and deviations are reported with reasons.
- The question is in a structured frame and the criteria follow from it.
- Every search string is recorded per database with its date and can be rerun.
- Screening was done by two people with agreement reported, and every full-text exclusion has a reason.
- The flow counts reconcile.
- Risk of bias is assessed with a named tool and actually affects the synthesis.
- Studies are pooled only where they are comparable, and the reason is stated either way.
- The certainty of the evidence is assessed and reported per outcome.
