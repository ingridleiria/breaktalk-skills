# Crisis and Incident Communications

In a crisis, silence is read as either concealment or incompetence, and both are worse than the facts. The job is to tell each stakeholder what they need, as soon as it is known, in the right order, and then to keep telling them on a promised rhythm until it is over. Speed, accuracy, and sequence matter more than polish.

## First hour

1. **Establish the facts as currently known**: what happened, when it was detected, who is affected and how many, what is being done, when the next update will come. Write them down with a timestamp; every later communication is built from this sheet, and it is updated as facts change. Separate confirmed from suspected.
2. **Name the roles**: the incident lead (runs the response), the communications lead (owns every outbound message), the decision-maker (usually the CEO), the legal contact, and the single spokesperson. Nobody else speaks externally.
3. **Sequence the stakeholders**, in the order they must hear, with the reason: those at risk of harm first (affected customers or users, if action on their part reduces harm), then those who will be asked (front-line staff, support, account managers, who need lines to take before customers call), then the board and investors (before they read it elsewhere), then the wider company, then partners, then press and public if relevant, then regulators on the statutory timeline where one applies (many breach regimes have fixed notification windows; legal confirms which).
4. **Issue the holding statement** to the audiences that need one now: what is known, what is being done, when the next update comes. A holding statement promises a time, not a resolution.

## Message rules

- Lead with what happened and who is affected. No preamble.
- Say what is known, what is not yet known, and what is being done to find out. Do not speculate on cause until it is confirmed.
- Say what the recipient should do, if anything.
- Promise the next update at a specific time, and deliver it on time even if the update is "no change".
- One version of the facts, adapted per audience in emphasis, never in substance. Contradictions between what customers and staff were told are the most damaging discoveries.
- Apologize for the impact when the organization caused it, plainly and once; legal reviews language around liability, but "we are sorry for the disruption" is almost always right.
- No blame of individuals, vendors, or customers in any communication.

## Audience-specific formats

- **Affected customers or users**: what happened, whether their data or service was affected, what the organization has done, what they should do, where to get help, when the next update comes. Plain language; a named contact.
- **Internal staff**: the facts sheet in brief, the lines to take, what not to say, where to route questions, and a thank you to the response team. Sent before the external message, minutes apart.
- **Board and investors**: facts, impact assessment (customers, revenue, legal, reputation), the response, decisions needed from them, the communication plan. Short and factual; the chair by phone first for a serious incident.
- **Press and public**: the spokesperson only; a short written statement from the facts sheet; no interviews until facts are stable; monitor coverage and correct factual errors quickly.
- **Regulators and authorities**: legal leads; the template and timeline follow the statutory requirement.

## The rhythm

- Internal war-room check-ins at a fixed interval (hourly at first, then every few hours), each producing an updated facts sheet and the next outbound updates.
- External updates on the promised cadence until resolution, then a resolution message, then a post-incident summary.
- A communications log: every message sent, to whom, when, by whom, with the version of the facts it carried. This log is the audit trail and the source for the review.

## Resolution and after

- **Resolution message**: what happened in full (cause, if confirmed), what was affected, what was done, what will change to prevent recurrence, and any remedy (credits, support, remediation steps). Sent to every audience that received an incident message.
- **Post-incident review** within two weeks: timeline, root cause, what worked and what did not in the communication, the gaps in the plan, and the changes made. Blameless in tone, specific in findings. Shared internally; a summary externally where trust needs rebuilding.

## Preparedness (before any incident)

A one-page plan kept current: roles and deputies with contact details, the stakeholder sequence, holding statement templates per incident type, the approval path (short), the channels and who has access, the regulatory notification requirements by jurisdiction, and a quarterly tabletop exercise. The plan is what allows the first hour to take an hour.

## Conduct

- Speed over polish, accuracy over speed. Never send a fact that has not been confirmed as a fact.
- This skill covers communication; legal, security, and technical response are led by their owners, and legal reviews external statements on serious incidents.
- Protect affected individuals' privacy in every message.

## Quality bar

- The facts sheet exists, is timestamped, and every message traces to a version of it.
- Stakeholders were told in the right order, with staff briefed before customers called.
- Every message promised a next update time, and the promise was kept.
- The communications log is complete.
- A review was held and its changes adopted.
