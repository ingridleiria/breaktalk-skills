# Chief of Staff at Series C

The role narrows and gets more senior. At earlier stages it covered whatever had no owner; here it usually carries a named mandate, and the rest of the surface belongs to people who are genuinely better at it than you are.

This is the inflection point where companies either learn to run at scale or spend three years discovering that the thing that got them here does not work any more.

## The company's actual problem

Everything that was implicit now has to be explicit, and every time something becomes explicit it gets slower. Approval chains appear. Legal is involved earlier. Finance wants the business case. Each individual addition is defensible and the sum is a company that takes six weeks to do what used to take a day.

The job is to make the necessary parts of that explicit and refuse the unnecessary parts, which requires knowing the difference and being senior enough to say so.

## The mandate

At this stage, ask for the mandate in writing and keep it to one or two things. Typical shapes: run the annual planning and strategy process; carry a transformation programme such as a market entry, a pricing change, or a platform migration; own executive team effectiveness; prepare the company for a transaction or a listing; integrate an acquisition.

Without a written mandate the role dissolves into whatever the chief executive did not do that week, which at this size is a full-time job that produces nothing anyone can point to at the end of the year.

## What the role owns

- **The mandate**, whatever it is, run as a real programme with milestones and a steering rhythm.
- **The strategy and planning process**, at a scale where it now involves finance, functional plans, and a board approval.
- **Executive team effectiveness**: whether the leadership team functions as a team, whether the right things reach it, and whether its meetings decide anything. Nobody else can raise this.
- **Governance**: board and committee coordination, the material, the minutes, the calendar of decisions that must legally happen.
- **The chief executive's leverage**, which now means declining things on their behalf more than scheduling things for them.

## Professionalising without killing speed

Every process added at this stage should answer three questions before it is adopted. What failure is this preventing, and has it actually happened here. Who does it slow down and by how much. What is the exception path, and who can grant it.

A process with no exception path becomes a reason to route around the whole system, which is worse than the risk it was preventing.

Keep a small list of decisions that are deliberately kept fast and low-approval, and defend it. A company that has made everything careful has made nothing important.

## Governance gets real

Board meetings become formal, committees appear, and the company acquires legal obligations about what is recorded and when. `board-and-investor-management` covers the mechanics. What changes at this stage: directors join who were not there at the beginning, minutes become a document that matters, and there are decisions that must legally be made by the board rather than by the chief executive.

Keep a calendar of those and never discover one late.

## Complexity that arrives at this size

Multiple entities, multiple countries, multiple currencies, employment law in places nobody has worked before, and data protection obligations that vary by jurisdiction. None of this is exciting and all of it becomes expensive when discovered late.

Acquisitions become plausible, and integration is done badly by default because it is nobody's day job. If one happens, it is usually this role's mandate for a year.

## What breaks at this size

The founding team. Some of the people who got the company here cannot do the job at this size, and everybody can see it before anybody says it. This is the most painful thing at this stage and the most damaging to leave alone.

Culture drift. The company hires enough people who never met the founders that the culture becomes whatever the managers do. It is set by behaviour at this point, not by values on a wall.

Reporting theatre. The number of dashboards grows and the amount of understanding does not.

Politics. Genuine competition for resource and scope appears for the first time. It is normal, it is not a moral failing, and the answer is explicit decision rights rather than appeals to alignment.

## Decide alone, or escalate

Decide alone: everything inside the mandate, the governance calendar, the shape of board material, the planning process, and what reaches the chief executive.

Escalate: executive hires and exits, anything with material legal or financial exposure, anything that changes the strategy, and any judgement about a member of the leadership team.

## When the stage has ended

You are making decisions in place of the chief executive as a matter of routine rather than exception. The company is preparing for a listing or a sale. Governance is continuous rather than quarterly. Read `cos-at-series-d`.

## Quality bar

- The mandate is written down, scoped to one or two things, and agreed with the chief executive.
- Every new process names the failure it prevents, who it slows, and its exception path.
- A short list of deliberately fast decisions exists and is defended.
- The governance calendar covers everything the board must legally decide, and nothing on it is discovered late.
- Executive team effectiveness is raised as a subject, since nobody else will raise it.
- Where a founding leader has outgrown their role, the observation reaches the chief executive rather than the corridor.
