# Operating Cadence Design

An operating cadence is the set of recurring moments where a company looks at itself, decides, and adjusts. When it is designed well, information flows up without chasing, decisions get made at the right altitude, and the calendar stops being the enemy. When it is absent, everything becomes an ad hoc meeting and the Chief of Staff spends the week as a human router.

## Diagnose before designing

Start with the current state, honestly:

1. **Inventory the recurring meetings**: name, frequency, duration, attendees, stated purpose, actual purpose, and the artifact it produces (if any). Multiply duration by attendees for weekly hours consumed.
2. **Map the decision flow**: for the last five significant decisions, where were they actually made, who was in the room, and how long did they take from question to decision. Cadence problems show up as decisions made in hallways or never made at all.
3. **Find the gaps and the duplicates**: information that reaches leadership late or by accident; topics discussed in three meetings and owned by none; meetings that exist because a tracker is not trusted.
4. **Name the company's stage and shape**: a 15-person startup, a 65-person scale-up across four continents, and a 400-person division need different rhythms. Distributed teams across time zones need more written cadence and fewer synchronous meetings.

Present the diagnosis as a one-page finding before proposing anything.

## Design principles

- **Every meeting produces an artifact.** If nothing is written afterwards, the meeting did not need to happen synchronously.
- **Written before spoken.** Status is written and read beforehand; meeting time is for exceptions, decisions, and debate. See the weekly-status-update skill in this library.
- **Altitude matches frequency.** Weekly for execution and blockers, monthly for metrics and resourcing, quarterly for strategy and priorities, annually for direction and budget. A weekly meeting that discusses strategy and a quarterly that discusses tickets are both broken.
- **One owner per meeting**, responsible for the agenda, the pre-read, the record, and the follow-up.
- **Fewer, longer, better.** One well-run 90-minute leadership meeting beats three 45-minute ones.
- **Time zones are a design constraint, not an afterthought.** Rotate the pain for synchronous meetings, and move everything that can be asynchronous to writing.

## A reference cadence for a scale-up

Adapt, do not copy:

| Cycle | Moment | Attendees | Duration | Artifact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daily | Written check-in in the team channel | Each team | 5 min async | Three-line updates |
| Weekly | Leadership meeting: exceptions, decisions, blockers | CEO and direct reports | 60 to 90 min | Decision log entries, action list |
| Weekly | CEO one-on-ones | CEO with each report | 30 min | Private notes, follow-ups |
| Weekly | Chief of Staff rollup to CEO | CoS, CEO | Written | Leadership status rollup |
| Biweekly | Pipeline or product review | Function lead and team | 60 min | Updated forecast or roadmap |
| Monthly | Metrics and financial review | Leadership, finance | 90 min | Dashboard, variance notes, reforecast |
| Monthly | All-hands | Whole company | 45 min | Recording, Q&A log |
| Quarterly | Priority setting (OKRs or equivalent) | Leadership, then teams | Half day plus a week of cascade | Quarterly priorities document |
| Quarterly | Board meeting | Board, CEO, CoS, finance | Half day | Board pack, minutes, follow-up list |
| Annually | Strategy and budget offsite | Leadership | Two days | Annual plan, budget, top priorities |
| Annually | Performance and compensation cycle | Managers, people team | Multi-week process | Reviews, decisions |

## The design deliverable

1. The diagnosis (one page).
2. The proposed cadence as a table like the one above, with every meeting's owner, artifact, and the meeting it replaces or absorbs.
3. The weekly hours before and after, so the saving is visible.
4. Agenda templates for the two or three most important meetings, with time allocations.
5. A rollout plan: which meetings change first, who is told and how, and the date the new rhythm starts. Change the cadence once, publicly, rather than in pieces.
6. A review date, eight to twelve weeks out, to check what is working.

## Quality bar

- Every recurring meeting on the calendar has a named owner and an artifact.
- Weekly leadership time is spent on decisions and exceptions, verified by the last four agendas.
- Decisions have a home and a log.
- Total recurring meeting hours went down or the increase is justified in writing.
