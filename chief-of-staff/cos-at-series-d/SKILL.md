# Chief of Staff at Series D and Beyond

The role stops advising and starts acting. Deep organisational knowledge, accumulated over years, makes it possible to decide in place of the chief executive on defined matters. That is either the most useful version of the job or the most dangerous one, and the difference is entirely whether the authority is written down.

## The company's actual problem

The chief executive's attention is now the scarcest resource in a very large system, and most of what reaches them should not. At the same time the company is subject to obligations it did not have before: to a board with real independence, to regulators, to acquirers or public markets, and to thousands of employees whose careers depend on decisions made three layers above them.

The job is to make the chief executive's attention land only where it must, and to make sure the things they are not looking at are actually being handled.

## Delegated authority, written down

Get it in writing and keep it narrow and specific. What decisions can be made in their name, up to what financial and reputational threshold, in what areas, and what must always come back to them. Who is told when the authority is used. What the escalation path is when a leader disagrees with a decision made under it.

Without this, the role becomes a shadow executive team, and functional leaders will treat it as one, correctly. Power without accountability is the characteristic failure of the role at this stage and it ends the same way every time.

Review it every six months. Authority granted for one situation quietly persists into others.

## What the role owns

- **Whatever the chief executive would personally own and cannot**, decided deliberately rather than by drift.
- **The company's largest structural change**: a listing, a transaction, an integration, a restructuring, a new operating model.
- **Governance at full weight**: board, committees, statutory obligations, disclosure discipline, and the calendar of decisions that must legally happen at a specific time.
- **Executive team composition and effectiveness**, in partnership with the people function.
- **Succession**, including the uncomfortable version, since a company at this size that cannot name a successor for each executive has a governance problem the board will eventually raise.

## Listing or transaction readiness

This is a multi-year state, not a project. What it requires: financial reporting that closes fast and reliably; internal controls that are documented and tested; a board with the right independent composition and functioning committees; disclosure discipline, meaning the company says the same thing internally and externally; equity and cap table hygiene; and a management team that can survive due diligence and answer consistently.

The failure mode is treating it as a project that starts twelve months out. Every item above takes longer to fix than the window allows, and the ones that stall a process are almost always the boring ones.

## Portfolio governance

At this size the company is several businesses in several places. The work is deciding what is genuinely common and what should be allowed to differ, and being honest that the answer is not always the same as the answer that makes reporting easier.

Standardise where it materially reduces risk or cost. Allow variation where local reality demands it. Insist on one thing above all: a common definition of the metrics, because a company that cannot add up its own numbers across units cannot be run, cannot be diligenced, and cannot be listed.

## What breaks at this stage

Distance from reality. Everything reaching you has been through three layers of summarising, each of which improved the news slightly. The only remedy is contact with the actual work, which has to be scheduled or it will not happen.

The founder problem, in reverse. A founder chief executive at this size may be doing a job they neither want nor are suited to, and the board is thinking about it before anybody says it out loud.

Change fatigue. The organisation has absorbed several reorganisations and the next one costs more in trust than it returns in structure.

Institutional memory loss. The people who remember why things are the way they are have left. A decision log kept over years is worth more here than anywhere else.

## Decide alone, or escalate

Decide alone: everything inside the written authority, governance operations, the shape and content of board material, the sequencing of the mandate.

Escalate: anything outside the written authority, without exception, including when speed argues for it. Anything affecting an executive. Anything with material legal, regulatory, or disclosure consequence. And anything the chief executive would want to hear from you before hearing from someone else, which is a judgement that improves with time and should be exercised generously.

## How the role ends

At this stage it usually resolves in one of three ways: into an executive role with a function, into the transformation it carried becoming a permanent business, or out of the company. All three are normal. The one to avoid is remaining indefinitely as a proxy with no accountability, because the role stops developing and the organisation stops respecting it.

Plan the ending deliberately, and say to the chief executive what you are aiming at. A role built entirely on proximity is fragile, and everyone knows it.

## Quality bar

- The delegated authority is written, scoped, reviewed every six months, and never exceeded.
- Decisions made in the chief executive's name are logged and they are told.
- Transaction or listing readiness is treated as a continuous state, with the boring items fixed first.
- Metric definitions are common across units, and reconcile.
- Contact with the actual work is scheduled rather than intended.
- The end of the role is planned deliberately rather than allowed to drift.
