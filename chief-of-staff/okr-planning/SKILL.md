# OKR Planning

Goal systems fail in one of two ways: too many goals, so nothing is prioritized, or goals written as tasks, so nothing is measured. This skill produces a small set of objectives with key results that could only be achieved by the outcome actually changing, then keeps them alive through the quarter.

## Writing objectives

- Three to five company objectives per year, two to four per quarter, and no more. If a sixth is proposed, ask which of the five it replaces.
- An objective is a qualitative statement of what must be true by the end of the period, in plain language a new hire understands: "Enterprise customers renew because the product is embedded in their workflow." It is not a metric and not a project.
- Each objective has an owner, a single named person, even when the work spans teams.
- Test: if every key result under it hits 100%, has the company's situation actually changed? If not, the objective is a to-do list wearing a costume.

## Writing key results

Two to four per objective. Each key result is a number with a baseline, a target, and a date:

- **Outcome, not activity.** "Launch the new onboarding flow" is a project. "Time to first value for new enterprise accounts falls from 21 days to 10" is a key result. Projects are listed separately as the bets made to move the key result.
- **Baseline stated.** A target without a current value is a wish. If the baseline is unknown, the first key result is to measure it.
- **Measurable by a named source**, updated at a stated frequency, so scoring is not an argument.
- **Ambitious and honest.** Stretch targets are fine if everyone knows they are stretch; a 70% score on a stretch target should be treated as success and said so in advance. Commit targets (must hit) are labeled as such.
- **Owned by one person**, who may not be the objective owner.

Rewrite every proposed key result through this filter and show the before and after.

## The cascade

Company objectives are set first by leadership. Teams then write their own objectives that ladder up, answering "what must my team make true for the company objective to happen?" rather than copying the company key results downward. Rules:

- Each team objective names the company objective it serves.
- Teams may have one local objective that serves no company objective, if it is necessary to keep the lights on; more than one signals the company objectives missed something.
- The cascade finishes within a week of company objectives being published. Planning that takes a month eats the quarter.
- Dependencies between teams are written into the key results ("requires the data team to deliver X by week 3"), and the dependent team has agreed.

## Scoring and review

- Score at quarter end on a 0.0 to 1.0 scale per key result, based on the measured number, not on effort. Objective score is the average, with commentary.
- Mid-quarter check: each key result marked on track, at risk, or off track, with the one action that changes the trajectory. This is the intervention point; end-of-quarter scoring is only a record.
- Retrospective: for each objective, what the scores mean, what was learned, and whether the objective continues, changes, or closes. Carry-overs are decided deliberately, not by default.

## Deliverables

1. The objective set with owners, in one page.
2. Key results table: objective, key result, baseline, target, date, owner, source of measurement, commit or stretch.
3. Projects and bets mapped to key results (so people know what to do on Monday).
4. Cascade template for teams and the cascade deadline.
5. The scoring rubric and the review calendar.

## Quality bar

- Nobody can find a task disguised as a key result.
- Every key result has a baseline and a measurement source.
- Every objective and key result has one named owner.
- The full set fits on one page and a new hire could explain the company's quarter from it.
