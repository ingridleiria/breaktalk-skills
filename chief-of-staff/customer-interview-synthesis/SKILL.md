# Customer Interview Synthesis

Ten interviews produce a hundred anecdotes and one or two findings. Synthesis is the discipline of getting from the first to the second without letting the loudest interviewee, or the analyst's prior belief, decide what was found. The method is mechanical on purpose.

## Step 1: Inventory the inputs

List every source: interview date, participant role and segment (company size, industry, persona, customer or prospect, tenure), format (notes, transcript, survey, ticket), and who conducted it. State the total, the segment coverage, and the gaps ("no interviews with procurement roles; three of ten from one industry"). Coverage determines what the synthesis can claim.

## Step 2: Code the material

Read every source in full. Tag each meaningful statement with:

- A **theme code** (created inductively as they appear, merged as they converge; aim for fifteen to thirty codes, then group into five to eight themes).
- The **source** and segment.
- The **type**: a problem, a need, a workaround, a praise, a request, a decision criterion, a competitor mention, a quote about willingness to pay.
- **Intensity**: mentioned in passing, discussed at length, or described as decisive.

Keep the verbatim text attached to every tag. Synthesis without quotes becomes the analyst's opinion.

## Step 3: Count and weigh

For each theme: how many sources raised it (frequency), how many did so unprompted, how many described it as decisive (intensity), and which segments it concentrates in. A theme raised by three of ten interviewees but decisive for all three is a different finding from one raised by eight in passing. Report both dimensions; never only the count.

Look deliberately for:

- **Contradictions**: segments or individuals who said the opposite. Explain the split rather than averaging it away.
- **Absences**: what nobody mentioned that the team expected. Absence in ten interviews is evidence.
- **Workarounds**: what people do today to cope; these are the most reliable indicator of a real problem.
- **Language**: the words customers use for the problem and the product, which should become the organization's words.

## Step 4: Findings

Five to eight findings, each written as a claim with its evidence:

- The finding in one sentence, stated as what is true for whom ("Mid-market finance teams abandon onboarding when the first integration takes more than a day").
- Frequency and intensity with the counts.
- Two or three verbatim quotes with segment attribution (anonymized as agreed with participants).
- The segment pattern and any contradiction.
- Confidence: high, medium, or low, based on coverage and consistency.

Findings are ordered by the combination of intensity and strategic relevance, not by frequency alone.

## Step 5: Implications

For the decision the research serves (roadmap, positioning, pricing, sales process), the two to four implications each finding supports, phrased as options with trade-offs rather than mandates, and the finding's confidence carried forward. Where the evidence does not support a decision the team wants to make, say so and state what further research would.

## The readout

A short document or deck: coverage and method in a quarter page, the findings with quotes, the contradictions and absences, the implications, and the next research needed. Quotes are the persuasive content; a readout with numbers but no voices does not change minds, and one with voices but no numbers does not deserve to.

## Conduct

- Read everything; sampling the transcripts is the failure this skill prevents.
- Quotes are exact and attributed to a segment; never improved.
- The analyst's prior hypothesis is written down before coding, so its influence is visible.
- Small samples are described as small; ten interviews support directional findings, not percentages presented as population estimates.
- Participant identities and confidential details are protected in every output.

## Quality bar

- Every finding has counts, intensity, quotes, and a confidence level.
- Contradictions and absences are reported.
- Coverage gaps are stated up front.
- Implications are tied to the decision and carry the finding's confidence.
