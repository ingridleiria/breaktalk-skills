# AI Adoption Program

Most companies buy tools and then wonder why nothing changed. Licences get issued, a few enthusiasts do impressive things, the rest of the organisation carries on, and a year later the only measurable outcome is the invoice. Adoption is a programme with a sequence, an owner, and a measurement, or it is a procurement event with a good story attached.

This work has landed with the Chief of Staff in many companies because it is cross-functional, nobody owns it, and it needs someone who can hold both the operational and the risk side. That does not make it easy, and it does not make an enthusiast an authority.

## Start with the work, not the tools

Audit where time actually goes before looking at a single vendor. By function, list the recurring tasks, roughly how much time each consumes, how repeatable it is, how much judgement it needs, and what the cost of an error would be.

The candidates are the tasks that are frequent, structured, low in consequence when wrong, and currently done by expensive people. The tasks to leave alone are the ones where an undetected error is expensive, where the judgement is the job, or where a person is accountable to a regulator or a customer for the outcome.

Ask what the actual constraint is. Where a process is slow because of an approval queue, faster drafting changes nothing, and the tool will be blamed for a problem it was never near.

## Sequence, do not scatter

Pick two or three uses, not ten. Choose them for a visible outcome inside a quarter, a willing team, and a measurable baseline that exists before anything starts.

Each pilot has a named owner, a defined scope, a before measurement, a success threshold agreed in advance, and an end date at which it is continued, changed, or stopped. Stopping is a valid outcome and saying so beforehand is what makes the measurement honest.

Do not run a pilot without a baseline. "It feels faster" is the most common finding and the least useful.

## The policy, written before the tools arrive

Short enough to be read, specific enough to be followed.

What data may go into which category of tool, with the confidential and personal categories named explicitly. Which tools are approved and how to request another. What must always have a human decision, listed as concrete cases rather than principles: anything with a legal or financial commitment, anything affecting a person's employment, anything sent externally in the company's name, anything a regulator would ask about. What must be disclosed, to customers and internally. Retention, and whether a vendor trains on the company's inputs, which is a contract question with a real answer. What happens when the output is wrong, because accountability stays with the person who used it.

Write it as one page people can follow. A policy nobody reads produces the shadow usage it was written to prevent, on personal accounts, with no logging at all.

## Procurement

Evaluate on the work, not the demonstration. Run the vendor's tool against real tasks with known good answers, and count the errors rather than the impressions.

The contract questions that matter: whether inputs are used for training, where data is processed and stored, retention and deletion, subprocessors, security certification, uptime and support, what happens to the data on termination, and price at renewal rather than at signature. `vendor-evaluation` covers the comparison and `business-agreements-drafting` covers the paper.

Check for overlap with what is already owned. Several existing platforms will have shipped the capability being bought.

## Build fluency, not licences

Capability spreads through people, not through seats. Run short, specific sessions on the tasks a team actually does, using the company's own material rather than generic examples. Pick a person per function who is genuinely interested and give them time, not a title. Keep a shared internal library of prompts and instructions that worked, so a discovery made once is available to everyone.

Measure adoption by task, not by licences issued. Weekly active use on a named workflow is a real number; seats assigned is a purchase.

## Measure honestly

Baseline, then measure the same thing after. Time on the task, error rate, throughput, and whether the person doing it wants to keep the tool. Report what did not work as prominently as what did, because a programme that only reports wins will not be believed the second time, and the second time is when the budget is larger.

Report to the leadership team quarterly: what was piloted, what the measurement showed, what was stopped, what is being scaled, what it cost, and what the risk position is.

## Quality bar

- The audit covers where time actually goes, before any tool is considered.
- Two or three pilots, each with a named owner, a baseline measured beforehand, a threshold agreed in advance, and an end date.
- The policy exists before the tools, names the data categories, and lists concrete cases requiring a human decision.
- Contract review covers training on inputs, retention, processing location, and renewal price.
- Fluency is built with the company's own work, and adoption is measured by task rather than by licences.
- Failures are reported as prominently as successes.
