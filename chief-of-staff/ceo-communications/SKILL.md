# CEO Communications

Communications in a leader's name are judged twice: on whether they say the right thing, and on whether they sound like the person. The second failure is more visible than the first. This skill calibrates to the leader's actual voice, decides the message before the words, and writes for the audience's questions rather than the sender's intentions.

## Step 1: Calibrate the voice

Collect three to five samples of the leader's real communication (emails they wrote themselves, transcripts of them speaking, past all-hands). Extract and write down:

- Sentence length and rhythm; whether they use short declaratives or longer reasoning.
- Vocabulary they use and vocabulary they never use; their names for teams, products, and priorities.
- How they open and close; whether they thank, whether they joke, whether they use numbers.
- What they emphasize: customers, craft, speed, people, mission.
- Formality and register, and how it shifts between internal and external.

Keep this as a one-page voice note for reuse. Without samples, ask the user to describe the leader's habits and mark the draft as uncalibrated.

## Step 2: Decide the message

Before drafting, answer in writing:

- **What must the audience know, feel, and do** after this communication. Three lines.
- **The audience's first question**: for an announcement, usually "what does this mean for me". The communication answers that question within the first quarter of its length or the audience stops listening.
- **What is not being said** and why (legal, timing, not yet decided). Decide it consciously; audiences detect omission and fill it with the worst case.
- **The hard part**: the fact or decision the leader would rather soften. State it plainly; softened bad news creates a second communication a week later.

## Formats

- **All-hands script**: opening on a concrete recent event (a customer, a number, a person), the state of the company in three to five points with numbers, the one theme the leader wants to land, what changes for the audience, recognition of specific people and work, and a close that returns to the opening. Written for speaking: short sentences, natural pauses marked, no slide-reading. Ten to fifteen minutes of script plus time for questions, with a prepared answer sheet for the likely ones.
- **Company announcement** (reorganization, leadership change, strategy shift, layoff, acquisition): the news in the first sentence, why in the second paragraph, what it means for the reader in the third, what happens next with dates, where to ask questions, and the leader's own stance in one honest paragraph. Coordinated with HR, legal, and the managers who will field the questions, who receive their briefing before the company does.
- **Internal memo on strategy**: the situation, the decision, the reasoning including the alternatives rejected, what the company will do and stop doing, and how progress will be visible. Written in prose; a memo that is bullets is a slide.
- **Message after a setback**: what happened, stated fully; what the leader takes responsibility for; what changes; what does not change. Short. Sent quickly.
- **Message after a win**: the specific people and the specific work, the customer or outcome, one sentence on what it proves, and the next thing. Not longer than the setback message.
- **External talking points**: three messages with one proof point each, the bridges from likely questions to those messages, the answers to the hard questions (prepared, not improvised), and the things not to say. On one page.
- **Thought leadership drafts**: the leader's actual opinion, with the evidence, in the leader's voice; not a company advertisement. See the newsletter-post-writer skill for long form.

## Writing rules

- The leader's vocabulary, not the communications team's. No "excited to announce", "journey", "north star", "double down" unless the leader really says them.
- Specific names, numbers, and dates; generalities read as evasion.
- Bad news early and plainly; the reasoning after; the support available after that.
- The leader reads the draft aloud before sending; anything they stumble on is rewritten.
- Coordinate timing: managers first, then the company, then external; leaks happen in the gap, so the gap is short.

## Deliverables

The message decision note, the draft in the leader's voice, the likely questions with answers, the sequencing plan for who hears what when, and, for spoken formats, the timing marks.

## Quality bar

- A colleague who knows the leader would believe they wrote it.
- The audience's first question is answered early.
- The hard part is stated, not softened.
- Names, numbers, and dates are specific.
- The Q&A sheet covers the questions the leader hopes nobody asks.
