# Pitch Deck

A pitch deck is not a description of a business. It is an argument that a specific commitment is a good idea now, made to someone who will decide in ten minutes whether to keep reading. Most decks fail because they explain before they have earned attention, and because their numbers cannot survive a single follow-up question.

Different from `board-deck`, which reports to people already committed, and from `ideation-deck`, which proposes an approach to a client who has already described their problem.

## Before the first slide

Fix four things, or the deck will drift.

- **Who decides**, and what they are actually assessing. An early-stage investor is assessing the team and the market. A corporate partner is assessing risk and fit. A client is assessing whether you can do it. The same facts get ordered differently for each.
- **The ask**, precisely: how much, for what, over what period, and what it buys. A deck built before the ask is known will hedge everywhere.
- **The one sentence.** What this is, for whom, and why it wins. If it takes a paragraph, the deck is not ready.
- **The objection they will actually have.** Name it now. It gets addressed inside the deck, on the slide where it naturally arises, not buried in an appendix.

## The structure

Ten to fourteen slides in the main deck. Everything else goes in the appendix.

1. **The problem**, stated as it is experienced by someone specific, with a number that makes it real. Not a category, a situation.
2. **Why now.** What changed that makes this possible or urgent, which was not true three years ago. A pitch with no answer here is describing an idea that could have been built at any time, and probably was.
3. **The insight.** What you understand that others in this space do not. This is the slide that separates a real pitch from a plan, and it is the one most decks omit.
4. **What you do**, in plain words, ideally with the product visible. One slide. If it needs three, the offering is not clear enough to fund.
5. **Evidence it works.** Traction, pilots, results, retention, whatever exists. Small and real beats large and projected. Where there is little, say what has been tested and what was learned, which is more credible than a forecast.
6. **The market**, sized from the bottom up: number of buyers times what they would spend, with both figures sourced. A top-down slice of an industry report is not evidence and experienced readers discount it entirely.
7. **The model.** How money is made, what a unit is worth, and what it costs to acquire and serve. Where these are assumptions rather than observations, label them.
8. **Competition, including doing nothing.** A positioning that admits real competitors is more credible than one that claims none. The status quo is always the incumbent and usually the winner.
9. **The team.** Why these people, for this problem. Relevant only where it is relevant; a list of logos with no connection to the problem is filler.
10. **The plan and the ask.** What the money buys, what milestones it reaches, and what will be true at the end of the period that is not true now.

Add a financial summary and a risks slide where the audience expects them, and stop.

## Rules

Headline titles carry the message. "Market" says nothing; "Two thousand mid-sized clinics spend $40,000 a year on this today" is the slide. A reader who sees only the titles should get the whole argument.

One idea per slide. Every number has a source in the footer. No projection without the assumptions that produce it, and no hockey stick that is not built bottom up from a driver the reader can question. Where you do not know something, say so, because a confident guess that collapses on the first question costs more than the gap.

The appendix is where detail belongs: cohort tables, the full model, technical architecture, regulatory analysis, detailed biographies. Build it, and refer to it, so the main deck stays at the pace of an argument.

## Before sending

Read only the titles, in order. Do they make the argument on their own? Then hand it to someone who does not know the business and ask them what the ask is and why now. If they cannot answer both, the problem is in the first three slides.

## Quality bar

- The ask is specific, and appears in the deck rather than only in the conversation.
- Titles alone carry the argument.
- Why now is answered, with something that actually changed.
- The market is sized bottom up, both inputs sourced.
- Competition includes the status quo.
- Every projection shows its assumptions; nothing is asserted that a follow-up question would break.
- The main deck is under fifteen slides, with the detail in an appendix that exists.
