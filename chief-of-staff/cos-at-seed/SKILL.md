# Chief of Staff at Seed

Twenty people, no systems, and a founder who is personally the routing table for every decision in the company. The job is not to optimise anything. It is to build the first crude version of things that do not exist, and to remove the founder from the middle of decisions that do not need them.

Speed beats elegance here by a wide margin. A rough tracker that people update beats a good system that arrives in six weeks. Everything built at this stage is temporary and should be built as though it will be replaced, because it will.

## The founder's actual problem

Not strategy. They have too much of that. Their problem is that every thread runs through them, they are the only person with full context, and they are making twenty small decisions a day that stop them making the three that matter. They are also probably fundraising, which removes half their week.

Everything below serves one test: did the founder get time back, and did the company keep moving while they were not looking.

## The first ninety days

**Weeks one to three: learn and list.** Sit in every recurring meeting. Read the last three months of the shared drive, the investor updates, and the board material if any exists. Talk to every person about what they are working on, what is unclear, and what they are waiting on. Write the list of everything that is broken, unowned, or repeatedly relitigated. Do not fix anything yet, except things that take under an hour.

**Weeks four to six: pick three.** From the list, choose the three items where the founder loses the most time and the fix is cheapest. Typically: a weekly rhythm that does not currently exist, a single place where work in progress is visible, and one process that is being reinvented every time it happens, usually hiring or customer onboarding. Build the crude version of each and use it once before showing it to anyone.

**Weeks seven to twelve: take something off their desk entirely.** One area, owned fully, that the founder stops thinking about. Investor updates, recruiting coordination, and vendor and tooling decisions are the usual candidates because they are time-expensive and judgement-light. Report on it rather than asking about it.

## What to build, in order

1. **The weekly rhythm.** One leadership meeting with a written agenda, decisions recorded, and actions with owners and dates. Thirty to sixty minutes. This one artefact fixes more than anything else on the list.
2. **One visible list of what is happening.** Whatever the team will actually update. A shared document beats an unadopted tool.
3. **The decision log.** Date, decision, who made it, one line of reasoning. Half of the chaos at this size is relitigating things nobody wrote down.
4. **The investor update.** Monthly, on a fixed date, same shape every time, sent even in a bad month.
5. **A hiring process that exists.** A scorecard, a consistent question set, and a decision meeting. Companies at this size hire on impressions and regret it for years.
6. **The numbers, in one place.** Five to eight figures the founder actually looks at, refreshed on a schedule, in a workbook where every figure has a source.

Nothing else. A seed company does not need an objectives framework, a competency matrix, or a strategy document with three horizons, and building one is a sign the role is filling its own time.

## Meetings to run, and to refuse

Run: the weekly leadership meeting, a short all-hands on a predictable cadence, and the hiring decision meeting.

Refuse, politely: standing meetings for coordination that a document would handle, project meetings without a decision, and anything recurring that has no owner other than you. At this size every recurring meeting costs a measurable share of the company's week.

## Decide alone, or escalate

Decide alone: process, tooling under a stated threshold, scheduling, the shape of documents, who is in a meeting, vendor selection within budget.

Escalate: anything about people, anything that changes what the company is building, anything a customer will see, and anything the founder has an unstated opinion about, which at this stage is most things worth asking about once.

The rule to hold: never surprise the founder in front of someone else. That single habit is what earns the scope to decide more later.

## What breaks at this size

The founder is still in every decision and secretly wants to be. Watch for this and name it kindly, because a Chief of Staff who builds systems the founder routes around has built nothing.

The first management layer appears at around eight people and nobody notices. Someone becomes a manager without being told, without support, and usually without wanting it.

Everything is verbal, so nothing survives a person being away or leaving. Writing things down is the cheapest insurance available at this stage and the hardest habit to install.

And the role has no boundaries, so it absorbs everything. Every system you build needs a named person it will transfer to, written down at the time you build it, or you will still own all of it at eighty people.

## When the stage has ended

Real functional leaders have been hired and own their numbers. Headcount is past thirty and the founder can no longer name what everyone is working on. Your calendar is full of things a function should own. Two or more of those means the job has changed and it is time to hand over what you built and read `cos-at-series-a`.

## Quality bar

- The founder has measurably more time, and can say what they got back.
- Nothing built is more elaborate than the company can maintain without you.
- Every system built has a named future owner recorded at the time it is built.
- Decisions are written down on the day they are made.
- The role has taken at least one area off the founder's desk completely.
