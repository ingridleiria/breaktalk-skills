# Chief of Staff at Series A

The money arrived, the headcount doubled, and everything that worked at twenty people is quietly failing. This stage is not about building; it is about rebuilding, and about giving away most of what the role owned at seed to the leaders now being hired to own it.

The founder no longer has full context and has not yet admitted it. Much of the job is making that transition survivable.

## The company's actual problem

Communication has started to fail across functions rather than inside them. Two teams are solving the same problem, a third is blocked on something nobody owns, and the founder is finding out about all of it late. Meanwhile the systems from seed, the shared document and the weekly meeting that fixed everything, no longer hold the traffic.

Around sixty people a second management layer becomes necessary whether or not anyone plans for it, and coordination fails until it exists. Expect that, and prepare for it rather than reacting to it.

## The first ninety days, if you are new

Same start as any stage: sit in everything, read everything, talk to everyone, and write the list. Then look for three specific things.

Where does work die between two teams. Which decisions get made twice. What is the founder still doing that a leader now exists to do. Those three questions produce the actual plan.

## Hand over what seed left you

Go through everything the role currently owns and sort it: transfer to a function, keep because it is genuinely cross-functional, or stop entirely.

Transfer recruiting coordination to the first people hire. Transfer the numbers to finance once finance exists. Transfer customer processes to whoever owns customers. Do it explicitly, with a date and a handover document, and then stop attending the meeting.

A role that only accumulates ends at this stage in resentment from the leaders whose territory it holds and burnout for the person holding it. What you keep should be the cross-functional work with no natural home, plus whatever the chief executive genuinely needs a proxy for.

## Rebuild, in order

1. **The operating cadence, properly.** Weekly leadership meeting, monthly business review, quarterly planning. Each with a stated purpose, a fixed shape, and a rule about what belongs in it. `operating-cadence-design` has the full method.
2. **Objectives that are few, owned, and measured.** The first quarter of doing this properly will be bad. Do it anyway; the second is better.
3. **The first real planning cycle.** Top-down target meeting bottom-up build, reconciled openly. Most companies at this stage plan by extrapolating, and the gap between plan and capacity is discovered in the third quarter.
4. **The dependency map.** Which team needs what from which, by when. Most cross-functional failure at this size is an unowed dependency rather than a disagreement.
5. **Internal communication that scales past the room.** Written updates, a predictable all-hands, and one place where decisions live. When people stop knowing what is happening, they invent an explanation, and it is usually worse than the truth.

Choose fewer rebuilds and finish them. Rewriting processes at the same rate the company grows means nothing is stable long enough to be adopted, which is the characteristic failure of an energetic Chief of Staff at this stage.

## Decide alone, or escalate

Decide alone: the shape of the cadence, planning mechanics, how information moves, tooling, and anything procedural.

Escalate: role and org design, anything about an individual's performance, resource trade-offs between functions, and anything where two leaders disagree. Do not adjudicate between functional leaders. Put the disagreement in front of the chief executive with both positions stated fairly, and let it be decided by someone with the authority to make it stick.

## What breaks at this size

The first-time managers. Individual contributors who were promoted for being good at the work now have reports and no training. This is the largest single source of attrition at this stage and it is nobody's job until someone makes it theirs.

The founder's shadow decisions. A decision is made in a corridor, three people act on it, and the leader who owns that area finds out later. Naming this is uncomfortable and necessary.

Meetings multiplying to replace lost context. Each new meeting is a symptom of information not flowing in writing; adding meetings treats the symptom.

Hiring ahead of the plan. Sequencing by when a manager asks rather than by when the hire produces output, which `annual-planning-and-headcount` fixes.

## When the stage has ended

There is a leadership team that runs its own functions and holds its own numbers. Headcount is past a hundred and fifty. Your work is mostly synthesis across functions rather than fixing inside them. The board has become a real governance body rather than a friendly investor call. Read `cos-at-series-b`.

## Quality bar

- Everything the role accumulated at seed has been transferred, kept deliberately, or stopped, with dates.
- The cadence exists and survives a busy week without being cancelled.
- One planning cycle has been run properly, including the reconciliation between the top-down target and the bottom-up build.
- Cross-functional dependencies are written down and owed by named people.
- Disagreements between leaders are escalated with both positions stated, never adjudicated by this role.
- First-time managers have support, or the absence of it has been named to the chief executive.
