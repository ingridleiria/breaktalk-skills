# Outreach Email

The difference between outreach that gets a reply and outreach that gets deleted is whether the sender demonstrably knows something specific about the reader. This skill puts the research before the writing and the reader before the sender.

## Step 1: Research the person and the account

Never write from the name alone. Build a short research note first.

**With ZoomInfo connected** (tools may be named like account research, contact enrichment, company signals, intent, scoops, recommend contacts):

1. Enrich the contact: current title, department, tenure, reporting line if available, recent job change.
2. Research the account: firmographics, recent news and scoops (funding, leadership changes, expansions, layoffs, product launches), and intent signals (topics the company is actively researching).
3. Check relationship history if a CRM or engagement tool is connected: prior conversations, emails, open threads, commitments made on either side.
4. Confirm the contact is the right person for this message. If the tools suggest a better-fit contact, say so before drafting.

**Without ZoomInfo**: use web search for the company's recent news, the person's public profile and posts, job postings that reveal priorities, and any earnings or press material. Say explicitly which facts are verified and which are inferred.

Write the research note in five lines: who they are, what their company is doing right now, one specific recent signal, the likely priority behind it, and how the sender's offer connects. If the fifth line cannot be written honestly, do not send the email; tell the user the connection is not there yet.

## Step 2: Choose the message type

Each type has a different job and a different shape:

- **Cold outreach**: one signal, one pain, one line of positioning, one small ask. Under 120 words.
- **Warm introduction** (mutual contact, event, shared context): name the connection in the first sentence, then one reason this is relevant now.
- **Post-call follow-up**: what was agreed, in their words; the commitments on each side with dates; the next step. Written from the call notes, never from memory of what should have been said.
- **Re-engagement** after silence: acknowledge the gap without apologizing for it, bring one new signal or piece of value, restate the ask smaller than before.
- **Executive-to-executive**: shorter still, peer tone, one strategic observation, one question.

## Step 3: Write it

Structure that works across types:

1. **Opening line** contains the specific signal from the research note, in a form that could only apply to this reader. "Saw the Series B announcement" is weak; "Your CFO mentioned on the Q2 call that expansion into Texas depends on onboarding speed" is the standard.
2. **The connection** between that signal and the sender's offer, one or two sentences, stated as an observation rather than a pitch.
3. **Proof**, one sentence, with a number where possible (a similar client, a result).
4. **The ask**, small and specific: a 20-minute call next week, a reply to one question, permission to send a two-page note. Never "let me know if you're interested".
5. **Sign-off**, no template warmth.

Subject lines: under seven words, specific to the reader, no clickbait, no "quick question".

Voice rules: first person singular, short sentences, no adjectives about the sender's own product (innovative, leading, cutting-edge are banned), no flattery of the reader, no exclamation marks, no bullet points inside a cold email.

## Step 4: Variants and rationale

Deliver two variants when the situation has competing goals (for example, create urgency versus build a longer relationship), label each by its goal, and include the five-line research note so the user can verify every fact before sending. One variant is enough for transactional follow-ups.

## Guardrails

- Every fact about the reader or their company is verified in this session or marked as an assumption for the user to check. Fabricated personalization is worse than none.
- Respect the source: information from enrichment tools or CRM is used to be relevant, never cited back to the reader in a way that feels like surveillance ("I see you were at X company in 2019").
- If the research reveals the timing is wrong (layoffs this week, an executive just left), say so and recommend waiting.
