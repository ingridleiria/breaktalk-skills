# Hiring Scorecard and Interview Kit

Hiring goes wrong in two predictable ways: the role was never defined beyond a title, so every interviewer measures something different; and interviews were unstructured, so decisions were made on rapport. This skill defines the role as outcomes before anyone is interviewed and gives every interviewer the same questions and the same rubric.

## Step 1: The scorecard (before anything else)

One page, agreed by the hiring manager and the people the role works with:

- **Mission**: the role's purpose in one sentence, from the company's point of view. "Own the operating layer around the CEO so that decisions are made once and executed."
- **Outcomes**: three to five things the person must have achieved by twelve months, each measurable. "Forecast accuracy within 10% by Q2", "Weekly leadership meeting running on written status by month two". Outcomes, not duties.
- **Competencies**: the five to eight capabilities the outcomes require, each defined in one line with an observable behavior. Split into must-have and strong-plus. Include the working style the team needs (independent operator, written communicator, comfortable with ambiguity), because those are the most common cause of a bad hire.
- **Constraints**: location or timezone, level, compensation range, start date.

The job description is written from the scorecard, not the other way round. It leads with the mission and the outcomes, states the competencies honestly, describes what the person gets (scope, exposure, growth), and states the constraints. No lists of twenty responsibilities, no "rockstar", no requirements that are not actually required.

## Step 2: The process

Design the stages, each with a purpose and an owner, typically:

1. **Screen** (30 minutes): motivation, constraints, and a check on the two most essential competencies. Kills mismatches early.
2. **Hiring manager interview** (60 minutes): the outcomes; past evidence of having done the closest equivalent.
3. **Work sample or case** (take-home of two to three hours, or a live case): the candidate does a bounded version of the actual work. This is the highest-signal stage; design it around a real outcome from the scorecard, with a rubric written before any candidate sees it.
4. **Panel or peer interviews** (45 minutes each, two or three): each interviewer assigned specific competencies, so coverage is complete and no competency is assessed by everyone at the expense of others.
5. **Reference checks**: structured, with the questions below.
6. **Decision meeting**: within two days of the last interview.

Total elapsed time target: under three weeks from first contact to offer. State it and hold it; the best candidates have other processes running.

## Step 3: Interview guides

For each stage, a guide with:

- The competencies assigned to this interviewer.
- Three to five questions per competency, in the past-behavior form ("Tell me about a time you...") followed by the probes that get to specifics: what was the situation, what did you personally do, what happened, what would you do differently. Hypotheticals are used only for role-specific judgment ("How would you approach...").
- **What a strong answer contains**, written next to each question, so interviewers score against a standard rather than an impression. Include what a weak answer sounds like.
- Time allocation, and five minutes reserved for the candidate's questions.
- A scoring section per competency: 1 to 4 with anchored descriptions, plus a mandatory note of the evidence for the score.

## Step 4: The rubric and the decision

- Scores from every interviewer are submitted in writing before the decision meeting, so the first opinion voiced does not anchor the room.
- The meeting reviews evidence per competency, not overall impressions. Disagreements are resolved by examining what each interviewer actually observed.
- The decision is against the scorecard: does the evidence support that this person will achieve the outcomes. A strong candidate who fails a must-have is a no; a candidate who is likeable and unproven on the outcomes is a no.
- The hiring manager decides; the kit makes the decision defensible.

## Reference checks

Structured, with the candidate's consent and with references who managed the person: the context of the work, the outcomes achieved, the specific strengths with examples, where the person needed the most support, how they handled a disagreement or a setback, whether the reference would hire them again and for what role. Listen for what is not said.

## Fairness and compliance

Every candidate for the role gets the same stages, the same questions, and the same rubric. Questions concern the role's competencies only; nothing about protected characteristics, family plans, health, or age. Employment law varies by jurisdiction; this skill designs the assessment, and legal or HR review covers compliance for the location.

## Deliverables

The scorecard, the job description, the process with owners and timeline, the interview guides per stage, the work sample with its rubric, the scoring template, the reference-check script, and the decision meeting agenda.

## Quality bar

- Outcomes are measurable and the competencies trace to them.
- Every interviewer has assigned competencies and a written standard for strong answers.
- The work sample mirrors real work and its rubric predates the candidates.
- Scores are written before the decision meeting.
- The process is identical for every candidate.
