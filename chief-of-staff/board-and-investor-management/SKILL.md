# Board and Investor Management

A board meeting is the visible part. The work that decides whether it goes well happened in the two weeks before it, mostly in one-to-one conversations, and the most damaging thing that can happen in the room is a director hearing something for the first time.

`board-deck` builds the document. This skill runs everything around it, and `investor-update` covers the regular written update between meetings.

## The calendar

Set the year at the start of the year: meeting dates, the committee meetings that precede them, the material deadline for each, and the annual items that must land in a specific meeting such as the budget, the audit, option refreshes, and compensation. Directors have other boards, and dates set late get declined.

Work backwards from each meeting: material circulated at least five days before, drafted and reviewed two weeks before, and the difficult items identified three weeks before, because those are the ones needing conversations rather than slides.

## The pre-read

The material is a pre-read, not a script. If it is being presented in full during the meeting, the meeting has become a status update and the company has lost the only three hours a quarter it gets from these people.

Circulate it far enough ahead to be read. Open with a short letter from the CEO in prose: what happened, what it means, what is worrying, and the decisions being asked for. Then the numbers against plan, with the variance explained rather than displayed. Then each decision item with the options, the recommendation, and the reasoning. Detail goes to an appendix.

State the asks explicitly on the first page. A board that does not know what is being asked of it will spend its time on whatever interests it most, which is rarely the thing that matters.

## Pre-wiring

Every significant item is discussed one to one before the meeting, with every director who will have a view. This is not politics, it is the difference between a board that decides and a board that defers.

Purpose of each conversation: surface the objection before the room, understand what would make them comfortable, and find out where they disagree with each other, which is usually the real reason an item stalls. Where two directors are opposed, get that into the open before the meeting rather than discovering it live.

Bad news is pre-wired first and individually, always. A director who learns about a miss from a slide will remember how they learned it for longer than they remember the miss.

## Running the meeting

Consent agenda for anything routine: approvals, minutes, previous actions, all pre-circulated and approved in one motion. Then the two or three items that genuinely need this group. Then a closed session without management, scheduled every time so that its occurrence is not a signal.

Time the agenda and protect the last item, which is otherwise where the important decision gets three rushed minutes. Someone other than the CEO tracks decisions and owners as they happen, and reads them back before the meeting closes.

## After

Minutes within a week, kept factual and short: attendance, resolutions passed, decisions made, and material dissent. Minutes are a legal record and a discoverable document. They record what was decided, not the discussion, and never the speculation.

Actions circulated separately with owners and dates, and reviewed at the next meeting as the first item so that follow-through is visible.

## Between meetings

The regular written update, on a fixed rhythm, sent even in a bad month. Especially in a bad month, because silence is read as trouble and the reading is usually worse than the truth.

Contact each director once a quarter outside a meeting, and know what each of them is for: the one with operating experience, the one who reads the numbers first, the one who opens doors, the one representing an investor with a different fund lifetime and therefore a different clock. Track commitments in both directions, including the introduction promised and never made, which is worth asking about politely and specifically.

## Difficult items

A miss, a departure, a restatement, a down round, a legal matter. Same shape every time: tell them early, in a call rather than a document, with what happened, what it means, what is being done, and what is being asked of them. Do not present a problem to a board without a proposed course of action, and do not present a course of action that hides how bad the problem is.

## Quality bar

- The year's dates and material deadlines are set in advance and hold.
- Material circulates at least five days ahead, opens with the CEO's narrative, and states the asks on the first page.
- Every significant item is pre-wired one to one, and bad news is never delivered first in the room.
- The agenda protects the decisions and puts routine items on consent.
- Minutes are filed within a week and record decisions rather than discussion.
- Between-meeting updates go out on rhythm, including in bad months.
