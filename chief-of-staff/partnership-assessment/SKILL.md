# Partnership Assessment

Partnerships fail more often from unclear economics and unowned execution than from bad intent. The assessment asks the questions the announcement press release skips: what each side actually gets, who does the work, how decisions are made, what happens when interests diverge, and how it ends. A partnership that cannot survive those questions on paper will not survive them in practice.

## Step 1: What is the partnership for?

State, for the user's organization, the single objective the partnership serves (distribution, capability, credibility, market access, cost sharing) and the metric that would show it worked at twelve months. If the objective cannot be stated, the partnership is being considered because it was offered, which is not a reason.

State the same for the counterparty, from evidence (see external-insights): their strategy, their gaps, what they have said publicly, what their recent moves suggest they need. A partnership works when both objectives are real and compatible; the assessment tests both.

## Step 2: Fit

- **Strategic fit**: does the partnership advance a priority the organization already has, or create a new one? New priorities need a stronger case.
- **Customer fit**: overlap of customers and buyers; whether the partner's customers are the ones the organization wants; conflict with existing channels or clients.
- **Capability fit**: what each side brings that the other lacks, stated concretely, and whether it could be bought or built instead and at what cost.
- **Cultural and operating fit**: decision speed, quality standards, communication norms; evidence from the negotiation itself, which is the first joint project.

## Step 3: Economics

Build the model with the financial-model-builder conventions:

- Revenue or cost effect for each side under base, upside, and downside, with the assumptions visible (volume, conversion, pricing, margin split).
- The investment each side makes: people, money, product work, opportunity cost.
- The split: referral fees, revenue share, wholesale pricing, co-investment, equity. Check that the split leaves both sides motivated in the downside case, not only the upside.
- Payback and the point at which the partnership is worth more than the alternative uses of the same resources.
- Concentration and dependency: what share of revenue or capability would depend on the partner at maturity, and what that dependency costs in bargaining power.

## Step 4: Operating model and governance

- Who owns the partnership on each side, by name, with time allocated.
- What each side delivers, on what cadence, with what service levels.
- The joint plan for the first ninety days and the first year, with milestones.
- Decision rights: what each side decides alone, what requires both, and the escalation path.
- Reporting: what data is shared, how often, in what form.
- Brand and communication rules: who may say what about the partnership, approval of joint materials.
- Conflict handling: competing products, overlapping customers, pricing conflicts, and the mechanism for resolving them.

## Step 5: Risks and exit

- The top risks: partner underperformance, strategic drift, acquisition of the partner by a competitor, data and IP leakage, reputational exposure, exclusivity that forecloses better options, dependency.
- Mitigations for each, and which are contractual (exclusivity limits, change of control clauses, performance thresholds, IP terms, data protection) versus operational.
- Exit: term, renewal, termination for convenience and for cause, transition obligations, what happens to shared customers, data, and IP. A partnership without a clean exit is a merger without the price.

## Step 6: The recommendation

A memo in the structured-problem-solving format: the objective, the fit verdict, the economics in one table, the operating model in one paragraph, the top risks and mitigations, the recommendation (proceed, proceed with conditions, pilot first, decline), the deal points to negotiate ranked by value, and the decision requested with a date. Where a pilot is recommended, define its scope, duration, success metric, and the decision at its end.

## Conduct

- Model the downside honestly; partnerships are sold on the upside.
- Evidence about the counterparty comes from research, not from their pitch.
- Commercial terms only; legal structure, antitrust, and regulatory questions go to counsel.
- If the honest conclusion is that the organization would be a junior partner absorbing the work, say so.

## Quality bar

- Both sides' objectives are stated with evidence.
- The economics show both sides motivated in the downside.
- Named owners, decision rights, and a first-90-days plan exist.
- Exit terms are specified.
- The deal points are ranked by value with a walk-away position.
