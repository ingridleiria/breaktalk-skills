# Chief of Staff by Stage

The title is stable and the job is not. A Chief of Staff at twenty people and one at six hundred share a reporting line and almost nothing else, and most of the unhappiness in the role comes from doing the previous stage's job well after the company has moved on.

Four archetypes, roughly mapped to funding rounds but actually determined by headcount, the presence of a real leadership team, and how much of the company the CEO can still hold in their head.

## Diagnose the stage first

The round is a weak signal. Ask instead:

- How many people, and how many layers between the CEO and an individual contributor.
- Is there a functional leadership team who own their numbers, or does the CEO still make functional decisions.
- Does the company have an operating cadence that survives a busy week.
- Can the CEO name every employee. Can they name every customer.
- Is the constraint finding what works, or scaling what already works.

A company that raised a large round while still run out of the founder's head is a seed-stage company with more money, and the role should be scoped that way.

## Stage one: the builder

*Roughly seed to early Series A, under thirty people.*

Nothing exists. The role is to build the first version of everything and hold the context nobody else has: the weekly rhythm, the tracker, the hiring process, the investor update, the vendor decision, the payroll setup. Breadth over depth, and speed over elegance, because a rough system used beats a good system built later.

**Owns:** the operating rhythm and its follow-through, cross-functional projects with no other home, recruiting coordination and often the first hires, investor updates, and whichever of finance, legal, and operations has no owner.

**Decision rights:** informs and occasionally advises. Almost nothing is decided independently, and that is correct.

**Success looks like:** the founder is less of a bottleneck, decisions get followed through without them chasing, and cross-functional issues resolve without asking permission each time.

**The trap:** becoming the person who does everything, permanently. Every system built at this stage should have a named owner it will transfer to.

## Stage two: the scale operator

*Roughly Series A to B, thirty to a hundred and fifty people.*

The systems that worked at twenty break at eighty, and the job changes from building to rebuilding. Communication starts failing across functions, ownership gaps appear between teams, and the founder's direct knowledge stops covering the company.

**Owns:** the operating cadence including leadership meetings and planning cycles, objectives and their tracking, cross-functional dependencies and the gaps between teams, the strategic initiatives that span functions, and the internal narrative.

**Decision rights:** advises, and influences. Runs process decisions independently.

**Success looks like:** planning is tighter, initiatives move from discussion into tracked execution, and executives stop spending their time collecting status.

**The trap:** rewriting processes at the same speed the company is growing, so nothing is stable long enough to be adopted. Choose fewer rebuilds and finish them.

## Stage three: the sounding board

*Roughly Series B to C, a hundred and fifty to five hundred people.*

There is now a real leadership team, which means the Chief of Staff no longer owns functions and instead operates across them. The work is synthesis: taking what each function reports and forming the view the CEO cannot form from inside any one of them. This is the stage where the role either becomes strategic or becomes a very senior project manager.

**Owns:** board coordination and the relationship between meetings, investor relations, the annual and quarterly planning cycles, change management during reorganisations, and the cross-functional initiatives that nobody's function fully contains.

**Decision rights:** advises, and sometimes decides on the CEO's behalf on defined matters.

**Success looks like:** strategy becomes executable process, and the discipline needed to scale arrives without killing the speed that got the company here. Both halves of that sentence matter and most companies lose one.

**The trap:** legacy thinking. Doing something because it got the company here is the most common reason growth stalls after this round, and challenging it is specifically this role's job.

## Stage four: the proxy

*Roughly Series D and beyond, including listing readiness.*

Deep organisational knowledge makes it possible to act rather than only advise. The role narrows, gets more senior, and often carries a specific mandate: a transformation, an integration, a listing, a new region.

**Owns:** whatever the CEO would otherwise personally own and cannot, plus governance, executive team effectiveness, and the programme that carries the company's biggest structural change.

**Decision rights:** influences, and decides in place of the CEO within a defined scope.

**Success looks like:** the CEO's attention is spent only where it must be, and the company's largest change lands.

**The trap:** power without accountability. The role at this stage needs an explicit written mandate, because a proxy nobody agreed to becomes a shadow executive team, and the functional leaders will treat it as one.

## The transitions, which is where the role breaks

Between stages, the previous version of the job stops working and there is no announcement. Three signals that a transition has been missed: the Chief of Staff is still the owner of a function that now has a leader, meetings are being run that a function should own, and the CEO is asking for things that a competent leadership team should be handling.

At each transition, three questions get asked out loud with the CEO: what am I stopping, who is it going to, and what am I picking up. Write the answers down. A role that only accumulates ends in burnout and resentment from the leaders whose territory it holds.

## When the role is wrong for the stage

Say so. A twenty-person company hiring a Chief of Staff to be a proxy will get an expensive person with nothing to synthesise. A four-hundred-person company hiring a builder will get someone drowning. Where the scope on offer does not match the stage the company is actually at, the useful thing is to name the mismatch before the hire, not after.

## Quality bar

- The stage is diagnosed from headcount, leadership structure, and where the constraint sits, never from the last round raised.
- Scope, decision rights, and deliverables are stated for the actual stage, not the aspirational one.
- What to stop doing is named as explicitly as what to start.
- Any proxy authority is written down and agreed, not assumed.
- A mismatch between the role on offer and the company's real stage is named plainly.
