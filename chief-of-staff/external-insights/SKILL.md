# External Insights

Insight is information that changes what someone will do in the next meeting. Everything else is background. This skill collects widely, verifies strictly, and delivers narrowly.

## Framing

Start by writing one line: who will read this brief, what meeting or decision it feeds, and when. A brief for a CEO walking into a partnership call at 3pm is a different document from a monthly competitor monitor. Confirm the frame with the user if the request is ambiguous.

## Source stack, in order of preference

1. **Primary**: the company's own material (press releases, annual reports, earnings transcripts, regulatory filings, job postings, product pages, pricing pages, leadership posts and interviews). Job postings are underused; they show what a company is building three to six months before it announces.
2. **Connected data tools** when available: enrichment services (ZoomInfo or similar) for firmographics, leadership changes, funding, technographics, and intent signals; CRM for relationship history; academic databases for research-based claims.
3. **Quality secondary**: major business and trade press, analyst notes, regulator statements, national statistics.
4. **Social and community**: LinkedIn posts by named leaders, conference talks, community forums. Useful for tone and priorities; weak for facts. Label accordingly.
5. **Aggregators and SEO content**: last resort, never the sole source for a claim that carries the brief.

## Verification rules

- Every fact carries its source and date in the same line.
- Numbers that matter to the decision are confirmed by two independent sources or marked single-sourced.
- Recency is checked explicitly: leadership, pricing, headcount, and funding facts older than twelve months are flagged as possibly stale.
- Distinguish what the company says about itself, what third parties report, and what is inferred. Never let inference read as fact.
- Nothing is fabricated. A gap is reported as a gap, with the best available proxy named.
- Privacy: research public professional information only. No attempts to find personal contact details, home addresses, or private-life information about individuals.

## Synthesis method

Collect into a working sheet with four columns: fact, source and date, confidence (verified, single-source, inferred), and relevance to the decision. Then answer these questions in prose:

1. What is this company or person trying to achieve right now, and what evidence shows it?
2. What changed in the last 90 days that matters?
3. Where is the pressure: what problem are they under, visible in hiring, financials, product moves, or leadership statements?
4. What does this mean for the user's decision or meeting? The one implication that changes the approach.
5. What would we want to know that the sources could not tell us? These become questions for the meeting.

## Output formats

- **Pre-meeting brief** (default for a named meeting): one page. Headline insight, five to eight facts with sources, three suggested questions for the meeting, one thing not to say (a sensitive topic revealed by the research).
- **Company snapshot**: firmographics, leadership, strategy signals, recent moves with dates, and the implication paragraph.
- **Industry or trend read**: what is happening, who is moving, what the numbers show, where the disagreement is (named sources on each side), and the implication.
- **Monitor update** for recurring tracking: only what changed since the last update, with dates.

Lead every format with the headline insight. Reference material and the full source list go at the end.

## Quality bar

- The reader can act on the first paragraph alone.
- Every claim traces to a named, dated source.
- The brief contains at least one thing the reader did not already know, or says honestly that public information adds nothing new.
- No adjectives doing the work of evidence: "fast-growing", "leading", "innovative" are replaced by numbers or removed.
