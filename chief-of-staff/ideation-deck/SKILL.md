# Ideation Deck

An ideation deck exists to get a decision on an idea that does not have one yet. It is not a brainstorm dump and it is not a business plan. Its job is to make the idea concrete enough to be judged, honest enough to be trusted, and short enough to be read before the meeting.

## Before building anything

Establish four facts with the user, in this order:

1. **The decision the deck asks for.** Approve a pilot, fund a phase, assign an owner, kill it. If the user cannot name the decision, the deck is premature; help them name it first.
2. **The audience and their objection.** Every audience has one objection they will raise before anything else (cost, distraction from the core business, "we tried this before", risk to a key client). Name it now; the deck must answer it before slide five.
3. **What is known versus imagined.** Separate facts the user has (a client asked for this, a competitor launched it, the team already has the capability) from things they believe (the market wants it). Both belong in the deck, labeled differently.
4. **The time budget.** A ten-minute slot gets eight slides. A thirty-minute working session gets fifteen plus an appendix.

## Deck architecture

Titles are headlines that state the point of the slide ("Three clients asked for this in Q2", not "Client feedback"). A reader flipping only through titles should get the whole argument.

1. **The idea in one sentence.** What it is, for whom, and what changes for them. If this slide needs a second sentence, the idea is not yet clear.
2. **Why now.** The trigger: what changed in the market, the client base, the technology, or the team that makes this worth raising today. Dated and sourced.
3. **The problem it solves, with evidence.** Real signals: quotes from clients or users, a number from the pipeline, a competitor move, a cost the organization pays today. Belief is allowed here only when labeled as a hypothesis to test.
4. **What it would look like.** The concrete shape: a mock, a workflow, a sample deliverable, a screenshot of a prototype. Ideas become judgeable when they become visual.
5. **Options.** Two to three ways to pursue the idea at different levels of commitment (a two-week test, a quarter-long pilot, a full launch), each with what it costs, what it needs, and what it would prove. Include doing nothing and what that costs.
6. **The recommended option and why.** State it plainly. Decks that end on "options for discussion" are asking the room to do the author's job.
7. **What we would learn and by when.** The pilot's success metric, the review date, and the kill criterion. This slide is what earns the approval, because it makes the idea reversible.
8. **The ask.** One slide, one decision, the resources and the date.

Appendix as needed: detailed costing, research sources, prototype details, risks and mitigations.

## Evidence discipline

- Every number carries a source and a date on the slide, in small text.
- Market size claims follow the same two-method rule as market research: top-down and bottom-up, reconciled, assumptions visible.
- Client quotes are attributed (with permission) or anonymized with the client type and date.
- Where a claim rests on the user's judgment rather than evidence, the slide says "we believe" and the appendix states what would validate it.

## Building the file

When a presentation file is required, follow the presentation-building skill available in the environment for layout and file generation. Apply the organization's brand if one is provided, or a brand skill in this library; when none is available, default to a restrained palette (one dark neutral, one accent), a single sans-serif family, generous white space, and no decorative imagery. Charts follow the same discipline: one message per chart, labeled directly, no legends floating inside the plot area, no rainbow palettes.

## Quality bar before delivery

- Read the titles alone. They must tell the full story and end on the ask.
- The audience's first objection is answered explicitly, not avoided.
- The recommended option has a metric, a date, and a kill criterion.
- Nothing on a slide is there because it looked empty.
