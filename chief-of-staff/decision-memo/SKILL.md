# Decision Memo

Most decisions that drift are not hard, they are unowned and unwritten. The same question returns to the same meeting three times because nobody wrote down what was being asked, what the options cost, and who was actually deciding. A decision memo is one page whose only job is to end that loop.

`structured-problem-solving` covers memo structure generally and `meeting-to-decisions` captures decisions made in conversation. This is for the decision that has to be prepared before the conversation.

## The shape

**The decision, as a question.** One sentence, closed, answerable. "Should we move support to a follow-the-sun model in the first quarter" is a decision. "Support coverage" is a topic, and topics get discussed rather than decided.

**Why now.** What forces this, and what changes if it waits. If nothing forces it, say so; not every question needs deciding this month, and saying so is a useful outcome.

**Recommendation, first.** State it before the analysis. A reader who agrees can stop, and a reader who disagrees now knows what to argue with. Burying the recommendation under the reasoning makes the reader do the work of finding the point.

**Options, two to four, including doing nothing.** For each: what it is in one sentence, what it costs in money and people and time, what it gives up, and what has to be true for it to work. Doing nothing is always an option and is often the strongest one, so it is presented honestly rather than as a straw man.

Do not present three options where two are obviously unacceptable. That is not a choice, it is a recommendation with decoration, and experienced readers recognise it and trust the memo less.

**The reasoning.** Why the recommendation, in a short paragraph. What evidence supports it, and what evidence would change it.

**Reversibility.** Say whether this can be undone, at what cost, and how quickly. This is the field that decides how much analysis the decision deserves. A cheaply reversible decision should be made fast on partial information; an expensive irreversible one earns another week. Confusing the two is where most decision-making time is wasted, in both directions.

**Risks, and what would signal them.** Two or three, each with the early indicator that it is happening.

**Who decides.** One named person, with who is consulted and who is informed. Where the decider is a group, say what happens if the group does not agree, because that is the case that produces a fourth meeting.

**The date.** When this must be decided, and what happens by default if it is not. A memo without a date is an invitation to defer.

**What we would need to know.** Only if it would change the answer. A list of unknowns that would not alter the recommendation is a reason to decide now, and saying that out loud is more useful than gathering more.

## Length

One page. If the reasoning needs more, the extra goes in an appendix nobody is required to read. A decision memo that runs to five pages will be skimmed, and the part skipped will be the trade-off.

## The decision log

Every decision made goes into one log, kept where the team already looks. Date, the decision, who made it, the reasoning in one line, and a link to the memo.

The log exists to answer two questions that otherwise consume enormous time: was this decided, and why. When someone reopens a settled question, the answer is the log entry and a request for what new information has appeared. A decision can be revisited on new evidence, and should be; it should not be revisited because the person who disagreed has waited long enough for everyone to forget.

Review the log quarterly for decisions whose assumptions have since been disproved. Those are worth reopening deliberately, which is different from reopening by attrition.

## Quality bar

- The decision is a closed question, answerable yes or no or by choosing a named option.
- The recommendation appears before the analysis.
- Doing nothing is one of the options, presented fairly.
- Reversibility is stated, and the analysis is proportionate to it.
- One named decider, with the tie-break rule where it is a group.
- A date, and what happens by default if the date passes.
- One page, with everything else in an appendix.
- The decision is written to the log with its reasoning on the day it is made.
