# Investor Update

Investors read updates for two things: the trajectory of the numbers and the judgment of the founder. A good update shows both, in under five minutes of reading, in the same shape every time so that trend is visible without effort. Founders who send honest, regular updates get help when they need it; founders who go quiet get remembered for the silence.

## Fixed structure

Keep the sections and their order identical every period. Investors compare updates side by side.

1. **Headline** (two or three sentences): the period in one breath. Revenue or the primary metric against last period and against plan, the most important thing that happened, and the state of cash. If the news is bad, it is here, first.
2. **Metrics table**: the same six to ten metrics every period, with this period, last period, the same period last year, and plan. Typical set: revenue (ARR or MRR for subscription businesses), growth rate, gross margin, net revenue retention or churn, customers or active users, pipeline, burn, cash, runway in months. Define each metric once in a footnote and never change the definition silently.
3. **Wins**: three to five, each with a number or a name. "Signed X, a 40-seat contract, our largest to date" beats "great momentum in enterprise".
4. **Misses and what we are doing about them**: the same discipline. State the miss, the cause as best understood, and the corrective action with a date. This section is the one investors trust the rest of the update by.
5. **Product and team**: what shipped, what is next, key hires and departures. Short.
6. **Cash and fundraising**: cash balance, monthly net burn, runway, and, if raising, the status in factual terms (conversations opened, terms received, expected close). No hype and no optimism adjectives around fundraising.
7. **Asks**: two to four specific requests investors can act on. Introductions to named companies or roles, a candidate for a specific position, advice on a specific decision. Vague asks ("any help appreciated") get no help.
8. **Thank you**: name the investors who helped since the last update and what they did. This is how you get more of it.

## Frequency and length

- Monthly for early-stage companies, quarterly once the board cadence carries the detail, with a short monthly note between.
- Under 800 words plus the table. If it is longer, the founder is explaining rather than reporting.
- Sent on a fixed day. Late updates signal something is wrong even when nothing is.

## Writing rules

- First person plural for the company, first person singular for the founder's judgment ("I think the SMB channel is not going to work at our price point").
- Numbers exact where known, rounded consistently, with the period stated.
- No forward-looking claims without a base: "we expect $1.2m ARR by December, from $0.9m today, based on the signed pipeline in the appendix" is acceptable; "we expect strong growth" is not.
- Bad news written as plainly as good news. The tone does not change with the content.
- No confidential customer names without permission; use descriptors.

## Quarterly variant

The quarterly update adds a short strategy paragraph (what changed in our thinking and why), a summary of the board meeting's decisions if the recipients are not on the board, and a refreshed twelve-month outlook with the assumptions listed.

## Quality bar

- An investor who receives this every month can build a spreadsheet of the metrics table from the emails alone, with no definition changes.
- The biggest problem in the company appears in the first paragraph or the misses section, never discovered later.
- Every ask is specific enough to forward to someone.
