# Annual Planning and Headcount

Two documents that are usually built separately and then contradict each other. The plan says what the company will do; the headcount plan says who does it and what that costs. When they are built apart, the plan assumes people who arrive in month nine and the budget assumes they arrive in month two, and the gap surfaces in the third quarter when it cannot be fixed.

Sits downstream of `strategic-plan-and-action-plan`, which sets the destination and the bets, and hands to `okr-planning` for the measured objectives and `operating-cadence-design` for the rhythm that reviews them.

## The calendar

Planning fails on timing more often than on content. Fix the dates first, working backwards from the date the plan must be approved.

A workable shape for an annual cycle: strategy settled and communicated eight weeks out; functional bottom-up builds due five weeks out; reconciliation and trade-offs three weeks out; leadership decision two weeks out; board approval one week out; communication to the company in the first week of the period. Quarterly re-planning is the same shape compressed into two weeks and touches only what has changed.

Name who owns each step and what they hand over. A planning cycle with no named owner per step will produce spreadsheets in different shapes that cannot be added up.

## Top down meets bottom up

Both, always, and the reconciliation is the actual work.

**Top down** starts from the destination: the revenue or outcome target, the margin or runway constraint, and the resulting envelope for spend. It is a small number of numbers and it is set by the leadership team.

**Bottom up** is each function stating what it will deliver and what it needs to deliver it, in a common template so the numbers can be added.

They will not match. The gap is the plan. Do not close it by cutting every function proportionally, which is the default and the worst option, because it underfunds the bets and protects everything else. Close it by going back to the bets: fund the two or three things that have to work at the level they actually need, and take the shortfall from what has been named as not a priority.

Record the trade-offs made. In eight months somebody will ask why a team is under-resourced, and the answer should be a decision on record rather than an oversight.

## Headcount

Sequence by when the hire produces output, not by when the manager asks.

For every role: the outcome it exists to produce, the month the requisition opens, realistic time to hire for that role and market, the ramp before full productivity, fully loaded cost, and what happens to the plan if it is not filled. Fully loaded means salary plus employer taxes plus benefits plus equipment plus software plus recruiting cost, which is commonly a quarter to forty percent above salary depending on the country.

The month that matters is not the start date, it is the start date plus the ramp. A senior sales hire starting in month four with a six-month ramp contributes nothing to that year's number, and a plan that assumes otherwise is not a plan.

Check three things people skip: span of control, because a manager plan with three reports each is a plan to build layers; the ratio of managers to individual contributors, which drifts upward silently; and backfill, because attrition is a real line and leaving it out overstates every year.

## Cost, runway, and the pause trigger

Build the cost curve by month, not as an annual total. Hiring twenty people evenly and hiring them all in the first quarter cost very different amounts in the same year.

State the runway under the plan, and under two alternatives: a downside where revenue lands materially below plan, and a case where hiring is delayed by a quarter. Runway is the number the board asks about first.

Then write the pause trigger before anyone is optimistic: the specific observable condition under which hiring stops. Below a stated revenue level by a stated date, or above a stated burn multiple, hiring pauses except for named critical roles. Written in advance, this is a policy. Written in the moment, it is a crisis, and it costs the company its credibility with the people it just hired.

## Communicating it

The plan lands as three things. A short narrative saying what the company is doing this year and what it is not, which is the only part most people read. The objectives, owned. And each function's own version, which must be the same numbers.

## Quality bar

- The calendar is fixed with named owners per step before any building starts.
- Both directions are built and the gap is closed by revisiting the bets, never by cutting proportionally.
- Trade-offs are recorded with the reason, so they can be answered later.
- Every role has a start month, a ramp, a fully loaded cost, and a stated consequence if unfilled.
- Attrition and backfill are in the plan.
- Cost is modelled by month, runway is shown under a downside, and the hiring pause trigger is written before it is needed.
