# Chief of Staff at Series B

There is now a real leadership team, which changes the job completely. You no longer own functions; you operate between them. This is the stage where the role either becomes strategic or becomes an expensive coordinator, and the difference is whether you produce a view nobody else in the company can produce.

## The company's actual problem

Every functional leader can see their own area clearly and none of them can see the company. The chief executive is receiving five confident reports that do not reconcile with each other, and the truth sits in the gaps between them.

That gap is the job. Synthesis is not summarising the five reports. It is noticing that sales is forecasting on a pipeline that engineering's roadmap cannot support, and saying so before the quarter ends.

## What the role owns now

- **The planning cycle**, annual and quarterly, end to end. This is the largest single thing at this stage and it takes weeks.
- **The cross-functional initiatives** that no function fully contains and that therefore die without an owner.
- **Board coordination** and the relationship with directors between meetings, since the board is now a governance body rather than a supportive investor call.
- **Change management** through reorganisations, which start happening at this size and are handled badly by default.
- **The chief executive's information diet**: what reaches them, in what form, and how much of their week is spent on things a leader should have handled.

What the role does not own: any function. If you are still running recruiting or owning the numbers, hand them over, because doing so is the only way to have time for the work above.

## Synthesis, done properly

Once a month, take every functional report and reconcile them against each other and against the plan. Look specifically for: a number that appears differently in two places, a commitment one team is counting on that another has not made, a trend that is visible only when two functions are read together, and anything the plan assumed that is no longer true.

Write it as one page for the chief executive. Not a summary of what each function said, which they already have. The view: here is what these say together, here is where they contradict, here is the thing nobody is looking at.

This document is the clearest demonstration of the role's value at this stage, and doing it once a month for a year changes how the company is run.

## The second management layer

Somewhere around this size the company acquires managers of managers, and the failure is predictable. Directors who were promoted for being strong managers now manage managers, which is a different job. Information degrades across two layers, so what the leadership team believes and what is happening on the ground start to diverge.

Two cheap interventions. Skip levels, structured and regular, with what is heard written up and fed back. And a written rule about what decisions sit at which level, because the most common complaint at this size is that nobody knows who can decide anything.

## Planning at this size

The cycle is now a real project with a calendar, owners per step, and a common template so numbers can be added up. `annual-planning-and-headcount` has the method. Two things specific to this stage: the reconciliation between the top-down target and the bottom-up build has to be done in a room with the leadership team present, not by spreadsheet, because it is where the trade-offs are actually made. And the plan must name what the company is not doing, which at this size is the only way anything gets adequately resourced.

## What breaks at this size

Legacy process. Things are done because they got the company here, and challenging that is specifically this role's job. Ask of every recurring process: if we were starting today at this size, would we do this. Most will not survive the question.

Speed. The discipline needed to scale arrives and quietly kills the pace that got the company here. Both halves matter and most companies lose one. Protect a small number of decisions that stay fast, and be explicit about which those are.

The middle. Between the leadership team and the individual contributors, a layer forms where strategy arrives as a slide and leaves as a guess. If you want to know whether the plan is real, ask three people two layers down what the company is trying to do this quarter.

Meeting load. At this size a leader's calendar fills without anyone deciding it should. Audit it once a quarter and delete rather than reorganise.

## Decide alone, or escalate

Decide alone: the planning process, the cadence, what the board sees and in what shape, how information moves, and the sequencing of cross-functional work.

Escalate: any trade-off between functions, org design, anything about an executive's performance, and anything with a legal or financial commitment.

## When the stage has ended

You are being asked to act rather than to advise. There is a specific mandate on the table, an integration or a transformation or a listing. The company is past five hundred people and governance is becoming formal. Read `cos-at-series-c`.

## Quality bar

- The role owns no function, and everything functional has been handed over with a date.
- A monthly synthesis exists that reconciles the functional reports and names the contradictions.
- The planning cycle runs on a calendar with named owners, and the reconciliation happens in a room.
- The plan states what the company is not doing.
- Skip levels happen on a rhythm and what they surface reaches the leadership team.
- At least one legacy process has been stopped this quarter, on the evidence of the question about starting today.
