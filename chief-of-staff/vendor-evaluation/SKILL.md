# Vendor Evaluation

A vendor decision is a multi-year commitment made on the basis of a demo. The evaluation exists to replace the demo with evidence: what the organization actually needs, how each option performs against it, what it will really cost over the term, and what could go wrong. The output is a recommendation someone else can audit.

## Step 1: Requirements before options

Write the requirements before looking at any vendor, or the vendors will write them for you:

- **The job**: what the tool or service must do, stated as outcomes for the users, with the volumes and the integrations involved.
- **Must-haves**: the requirements that disqualify an option if unmet (security and compliance, data residency, integration with a named system, a capability without which the job cannot be done). Kept short; a long must-have list is usually a wish list in disguise.
- **Weighted criteria**: eight to twelve criteria covering functionality, usability for the actual users, integration, security and compliance, vendor viability and support, implementation effort, and cost, each with a weight that sums to 100 and a definition of what a score of 1, 3, and 5 looks like. Agree the weights with the stakeholders before scoring; changing weights after scoring is how evaluations get rigged.
- **Constraints**: budget ceiling, timeline, procurement rules, contract term preferences.

## Step 2: Evidence per option

For each candidate (three to five is the practical range):

- **Functional fit**: scored against each criterion with the evidence source: a hands-on trial by the actual users (strongly preferred), a scripted demo using the organization's own scenarios (not the vendor's), documentation, and reference calls. A score with no evidence is a guess and is marked as such.
- **Total cost of ownership** over the intended term (usually three years): licenses or fees with the growth in users or volume modeled, implementation and migration cost (internal time included at loaded cost), integration work, training, ongoing administration, support tiers, price escalation clauses, exit costs (data export, switching). Present base and a growth scenario. The list price is rarely half of the TCO.
- **Vendor viability**: company age, funding or profitability signals, customer base and churn signals, roadmap credibility, acquisition risk, support responsiveness in the trial. Use external-insights for the research; cite sources and dates.
- **Security and compliance**: certifications verified against the actual documents (not the marketing page), data location, subprocessors, breach history, contractual data protection terms.
- **References**: two or three current customers of similar size and use, contacted with a structured set of questions: what was the implementation really like, what does not work, how is support, what would they do differently, would they buy again. Ask the vendor for references, then find one they did not offer.
- **Contract review** (commercial terms, not legal advice): term and renewal mechanics (auto-renewal notice periods are the most common trap), price protection, service levels and remedies, termination rights, data ownership and export, liability caps; legal review for anything beyond commercial terms.

## Step 3: The comparison and the recommendation

- A weighted scoring table with evidence notes per cell, must-have pass/fail shown first, and the TCO row beneath. Sensitivity: if the top two are close, show whether any reasonable change in weights flips the result.
- A risk register per option: the top three risks, likelihood, impact, mitigation.
- A recommendation memo (consultant-toolkit format): the decision, the recommended option and why, what it costs over the term, the risks accepted, the negotiation points, the implementation plan outline, and the decision requested with a date.

## Negotiation points

From the evidence: the competing quote as reference, the term length traded for price, the escalation cap, the notice period for renewal extended, service levels with credits, implementation support included, a pilot or opt-out clause, price locks for growth tiers. Prepare the walk-away alternative explicitly; the second-ranked option is the negotiating power.

## Renewal decisions

For a renewal, add: the actual usage and value delivered over the current term (adoption data, outcomes achieved), the cost of switching estimated honestly, and the vendor's performance against the previous contract's service levels. Renewal by inertia is a decision; make it visibly.

## Quality bar

- Requirements and weights were fixed before scoring.
- Every score has an evidence source; trials were done by the real users.
- TCO covers the full term including internal effort and exit costs.
- References were checked, including one not supplied by the vendor.
- The memo names the risks accepted and the negotiation points.
