# Program Management

A program is a promise with a date on it. The Chief of Staff's version of program management has one goal: nothing important loses momentum when priorities shift. Everything below serves that.

## The four artifacts

Every program, whatever its size, runs on four documents. Create them at kickoff and never let them drift apart.

1. **One-pager.** Objective in one sentence, why now, definition of done, owner, date, budget or resourcing, and the two or three metrics that define success. If the objective cannot fit in one sentence, the program is actually two programs.
2. **Workstream plan.** Break the program into workstreams, each with a single named owner. Not a team, a person. Per workstream: milestones with dates, dependencies on other workstreams, and current status. Milestones are verifiable events ("contract signed", "v1 live to 10 users"), not activities ("continue development").
3. **Tracker.** One source of truth, ruthlessly simple: workstream, owner, next milestone, date, status (on track / at risk / blocked), and a one-line note explaining any non-green status. A tracker nobody updates is worse than none; keep it small enough to update in five minutes.
4. **Decision log.** Date, decision, who made it, and the one-line rationale. Half of program chaos is relitigating decisions nobody wrote down.

## The operating cadence

- **Weekly**: written status per workstream before any meeting, in three lines: done last week, planned this week, blockers. The meeting then only discusses exceptions, never goes around the room reading updates aloud.
- **Every meeting** ends with next steps captured as owner, action, date. An action without all three does not exist. Circulate within 24 hours.
- **Monthly or per milestone**: leadership review using the one-pager metrics. Lead with the headline (on track, at risk, or off track and why), then the decisions needed from the room. Never present status without an ask or an explicit "no decisions needed".

## Status writing

Status updates follow one shape: headline first, then evidence, then asks.

- Headline states the program's condition in one sentence, honestly. "On track" written five weeks in a row before a slip announcement means the earlier updates were false.
- At risk means at risk of missing a date or metric, with the specific date or metric named and the recovery plan stated.
- Blocked names the blocker, the person who can unblock it, and the date the block became binding. Escalation is a tool, not a failure.

## Recovering a slipping program

When a program is off track, run this sequence rather than adding meetings:

1. Re-verify the definition of done. Scope creep hides here.
2. Find the critical path: the single chain of dependent milestones that sets the end date. Effort anywhere else does not move the date.
3. Present leadership exactly three options with costs attached: cut scope, move the date, or add resources. "Work harder" is not an option; it is the plan that already failed.
4. Reset the plan publicly, once. Serial small slips destroy more trust than one honest re-baseline.

## Anti-patterns to name when you see them

- A RACI where more than one person is Accountable per row.
- Milestones expressed in percentages ("80% complete" is a feeling, not a fact).
- Status meetings that exist because the tracker is not trusted. Fix the tracker.
- Programs with an owner but no date, or a date but no owner.
